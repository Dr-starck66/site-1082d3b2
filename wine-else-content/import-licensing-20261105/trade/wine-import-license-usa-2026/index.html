Wine Import License USA: Federal Permits, State Rules and a 2026 Buyer Checklist

A practical US wine import license guide for businesses buying French wine: TTB basic permits, state requirements, labels, FDA filings and the first shipment.

Published November 5, 2026 · WINE ELSE editorial · Regulatory information, not legal advice

At a glance: A viable commercial shipment needs a named responsible importer, product-specific approvals where required, an authorized sales route and a documented total cost. The checklist below explains how to verify the relevant steps.

What does “wine import license” actually mean?

There is no single approval that authorizes every step of commercial wine importing throughout the United States. A business may need a federal Importer’s Basic Permit from the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB), applicable state authorizations and a legally compliant route to wholesalers or retailers. Product labels, food-import filings and customs clearance are separate workstreams. A producer’s authority to export from France does not automatically give its American customer authority to import. Start by identifying which legal entity will import, which entity will distribute, and in which states wine will ultimately be sold.

Choose the responsible importer before choosing a shipment

Record the US legal entity, its business address, owners or authorized officers, federal importer permit status and intended first port. Ask whether the importer will also act as a wholesaler; that can be a separate federal permit consideration. Identify the customs broker and the party responsible for customs entry and taxes. When a buyer claims to have an established importer, obtain the correct legal entity name and have the relevant professionals verify its permissions. A broker, carrier, distributor and importer are different roles even when the same business offers several services.

Federal TTB permit is only the first gate

TTB explains that commercial alcohol beverage importers require an Importer’s Basic Permit before engaging in the business. Its application packet lists the basic permit form and supporting records. A buyer should not plan delivery dates around an unapproved application. The product also needs labeling review where applicable. An imported wine label typically requires a Certificate of Label Approval (COLA), subject to the product category and exact legal requirements. Check label approval and permit status with the licensed importer rather than assuming a French back label meets US standards.

State rules can change the commercial model

A wine shipment is not legally ready merely because the federal paperwork exists. State alcohol beverage control laws affect licensing, distribution, shipping and retail channels. A state-specific market plan should name the state, permitted buyer type and authorized delivery route. For example, selling to a distributor for subsequent licensed retail sale is different from shipping directly to an individual consumer. Before promising exclusivity or a sales territory to a producer, check the applicable state framework and get qualified legal review on contract terms.

FDA and customs belong on the same checklist

Commercial wine shipments can involve FDA food-facility registration and prior-notice obligations, subject to applicable exemptions and the exact roles of the businesses concerned. A shipment may also require customs entry information, correct tariff classification, applicable duties, federal excise taxes and other fees. Have the importer and broker verify the requirements for the particular wine, alcohol content, bottle size, origin, route and arrival date. An earlier shipment’s clearance is not proof that the next vintage or format is automatically compliant.

Use a five-gate readiness test before paying freight

Gate one: confirmed importer identity and authorization. Gate two: review of the actual labels and any required COLA. Gate three: state license and distribution route. Gate four: FDA-related filings and customs data assigned to named parties. Gate five: approved landed-cost estimate and payment/Incoterm terms. Mark an item “pending” whenever the evidence is missing; do not describe a planned shipment as compliant until each applicable gate has been checked. For a small Occitanie producer, this disciplined approach is more useful than an optimistic shipping date.

Frequently asked question

Can a French winery ship directly to a US retailer without a US importer?

Do not assume it can. Determine the US importer of record, federal permit holder, state distribution route and required documents before agreeing to a shipment.
