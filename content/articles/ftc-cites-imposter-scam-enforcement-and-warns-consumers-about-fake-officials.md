The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and obtained more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement is a reminder that scammers may borrow an agency’s name—or a charity’s—to make a demand or prize sound legitimate. [S1][S2]

The enforcement figures describe the FTC’s broader efforts, not a promise that a particular consumer will receive money back. For an individual message or call, the practical question is whether the contact is asking you to do something the FTC identifies as a scam warning sign. [S1][S2]

## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the FTC highlighted its work against impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 FTC-imposter websites and enforcement actions resulting in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the rule took effect in 2024. The agency also described impersonation scams as the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions in losses. [S1]

Those numbers show the scale of reports and enforcement activity, but they do not establish that every impersonator has been identified or that a specific report will lead to reimbursement. The announcement is about agency action, not an individual claim process or a new deadline for consumers. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer advice gives a simple warning for anyone contacted by someone claiming to represent the agency: the FTC will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Treat a demand to move or hand over money as a reason to stop, not as proof that the caller has authority. If someone says they are with the FTC and urges you to transfer funds, withdraw cash, or buy gold for safekeeping, the FTC says that is a scam. Don’t follow those instructions based on the call or message. [S2]

A familiar organization’s name is not enough to establish that a pitch is genuine. The FTC recently warned about scammers impersonating charities, including calls telling donors they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. If a prize claim arrives after you donated, check the claim independently rather than treating your donation or the caller’s familiarity with the charity as proof. [S2]

Use a short decision path:

- **Is the contact claiming to be the FTC?** If it threatens you or directs you to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold and give it to someone, treat it as a scam. [S2]
- **Is it a charity or sweepstakes pitch?** If it says you won a charity-sponsored prize, pause and verify the claim through a source you locate independently. The FTC has specifically warned about this impersonation pattern. [S2]
- **Does the contact still seem uncertain?** Don’t let urgency or official-sounding language substitute for verification. The FTC provides a Report Fraud option for consumers who want to report suspected fraud. [S1][S2]

The FTC’s reported redress total may sound reassuring, but it should not change how you evaluate an unexpected demand. The agency’s announcement describes the results of enforcement actions; it does not say that reporting a scam guarantees recovery, or that a particular person qualifies for redress. [S1]

If you suspect an impersonation attempt, keep the decision focused on what you can verify: who contacted you, what they asked you to do, and whether the request matches the FTC’s warning signs. You can use the FTC’s consumer advice site to find its fraud-reporting option and scam guidance. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a suspicious contact, check these points:

- **The requested action:** A demand to transfer money to “protect it,” withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over is specifically identified by the FTC as a scam. [S2]
- **The claimed connection:** A caller’s claim to represent the FTC or a charity does not, by itself, verify that connection. The FTC warns about both agency impersonation and charity-related sweepstakes impersonation. [S1][S2]
- **The prize claim:** If someone says you won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes, verify the claim independently before acting on it. [S2]
- **The meaning of the enforcement figures:** The FTC reports 19 removed imposter websites and more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024; those figures are not a guarantee of payment in an individual case. [S1]
- **Where to report:** If you believe the contact was fraudulent, find the FTC’s Report Fraud option through its consumer advice site. Reporting is an escalation option, not a promise of a refund or other outcome. [S1][S2]
