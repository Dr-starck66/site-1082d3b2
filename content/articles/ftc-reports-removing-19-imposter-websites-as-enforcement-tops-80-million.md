The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. Those figures show the scale of the FTC’s response, but they do not mean every person contacted by an impostor will receive money back. [S1]

The FTC also says imposter scams have been the most reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. Its latest announcement highlights agency enforcement; the consumer-facing warning is more immediate: someone claiming to be from the FTC may be trying to pressure you into giving up money. [S1][S2]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, announcement, the FTC summarized ongoing work to combat impersonation scams. It reported taking down 19 FTC-imposter websites and cited more than $80 million in consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

The figures describe agency action, not a new consumer application or a payment deadline. The supplied announcement does not identify a universal claim process, say that everyone targeted by an impostor qualifies for redress, or promise that an individual report will lead to repayment. Treat the $80 million figure as the reported result of enforcement actions—not as a personal refund estimate. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

The clearest test is what the person asks you to do. The FTC says it will never threaten you, tell you that you must transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. If a caller, texter, emailer, or website claiming to represent the FTC makes one of those demands, stop treating the contact as a legitimate agency request. [S2]

Use a simple decision path before taking any financial action:

- **Is there a threat or urgent demand?** The FTC’s warning says it will not threaten you or require a transfer, cash withdrawal, or gold purchase to protect your money. Do not follow those instructions. [S2]
- **Is the contact asking you to hand over cash or gold?** That is among the specific behaviors the FTC says it will never demand. End the interaction rather than arranging a handoff. [S2]
- **Does the contact claim to be the FTC but avoid those exact demands?** The supplied FTC guidance does not establish that every other type of message is genuine. Don’t use a familiar logo, official-sounding language, or a website’s appearance alone as proof. Verify the claim through a contact route you locate independently, rather than relying on links or numbers in the message. [S2]

The last step is a cautious way to handle uncertainty, not a guarantee that a contact is genuine. The supplied guidance identifies specific red flags but does not provide a complete authentication checklist. If you are unsure, pause before sending money or sharing information, and use the FTC’s consumer advice site or its fraud-reporting option reached independently. [S2]

If you already followed a demand, focus first on limiting further harm: stop communicating through the suspicious contact and do not send additional money. Then use the FTC’s report-fraud option. The supplied sources confirm that consumers can report fraud, but they do not specify response times, an individual recovery process, or what outcome a report will produce. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message or acting on an alleged FTC instruction, check:

- **What exactly is being demanded?** A threat, a required money transfer to “protect” funds, or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone conflict with the FTC’s stated warning. [S2]
- **Where did the contact details come from?** Don’t treat a phone number or link supplied by the sender as independent confirmation. Find the FTC’s official consumer advice or reporting page yourself. The agency’s site identifies itself as an official government website. [S2]
- **What does the headline number actually mean?** The more than $80 million is described as consumer redress from enforcement actions since 2024; the announcement does not establish that you personally qualify or can claim a share. [S1]
- **What can a fraud report do?** You can report fraud through the FTC’s consumer site, but the supplied material does not promise a refund or specify what happens in an individual case. [S2]

The practical takeaway is to judge the request, not the claimed identity: stop if someone claiming to be the FTC threatens you or directs you to move money, withdraw cash, or hand over gold. Report suspected fraud through a route you find independently, and do not assume that an enforcement total guarantees personal reimbursement. [S1][S2]
