The FTC has drawn fresh attention to a scam category that remains common: people are targeted by callers, messages or websites that appear to come from the agency or another government or business entity. The key point is not that every unexpected FTC contact is genuine. Consumers should assume an FTC-impersonation demand is suspect until they verify it through independent channels. [S1][S2]

## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the FTC said it had removed 19 websites that impersonated the agency. The agency also said its enforcement actions had resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. [S1]

The FTC’s director of consumer protection described imposter scams as the top reported fraud category in recent years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. The announcement does not establish that the $80 million figure applies to every FTC action or to every consumer affected by a specific scam. It describes the amount associated with enforcement actions since the rule took effect. [S1]

The FTC guidance identifies several warning signs specific to agency impersonation. The agency says it will never threaten someone, demand that money be transferred to “protect” it, or tell someone to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over. Those instructions should be treated as an immediate stop sign, not a request to test by calling the number provided in the message. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

A request to act quickly is a reason to slow down. An impersonation scam may arrive with a badge image, agency name, official-sounding language or a URL that looks close to a government address. The source of the message can be copied or fabricated. A return address or caller ID does not prove that the person or business behind it is legitimate. [S1][S2]

Use this decision path before you respond:

- **Did the contact come out of nowhere, and is it asking for secrecy or immediate action?** Stop and do not follow its payment or account instructions.
- **Does it claim you must move money, transfer funds, withdraw cash or buy gold to protect an account or avoid trouble?** Treat that claim as a scam, based on FTC guidance that says the FTC never makes those demands. [S1][S2]
- **Are you being sent to a link or told to call a number from the message?** Do not use those directions for verification. Open a browser and enter the organization’s real public web address yourself, or use a number you find from an independent, official source. [S2]
- **Can you confirm the request through the legitimate organization, without using identifying information supplied by the contact?** If not, stop. A person connected to the FTC or a legitimate business should not require you to disclose or move money based only on an unsolicited message. [S1][S2]

The redress total is a measure of agency enforcement activity, not a promise that a payment is waiting for you. The FTC website may have separate refund-program information, but the announcement does not say when or how individual consumers will receive redress in these cases or that all loss claimants will qualify. Do not send fees or personal financial details to anyone who says they can release a nonexistent or pending FTC payment. [S1]

FTC guidance is clear about one category of fraud: FTC impersonators may threaten victims or ask them to transfer money, withdraw cash or buy gold. Any such demand conflicts with the FTC’s own description of how it conducts itself. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, sharing account details or installing software, answer these questions:

- **Who initiated the contact?** Record the claimed name, agency or business, phone number and website, but verify the organization separately. [S2]
- **What exact action is being requested?** The FTC says it will not threaten you or require cash, gold or a transfer to protect money. A demand of that kind is enough to stop. [S1][S2]
- **Where did the payment instructions come from?** Do not let a caller dictate the destination or payment method. Verify the intended recipient independently.
- **Can the organization authenticate the request without asking you to reveal sensitive numbers?** Contact information and the organization’s real website should come from a reliable source, not the suspicious message. [S2]
- **Is the person asking you to keep the contact secret or act within minutes?** Treat secrecy and urgency as warning signs and pause.
- **What evidence should you preserve?** Keep the original message, call details, payment records and the names or links shown. This is useful if you report the incident, although the announcement does not state a required reporting deadline. [S1]

If you believe you have been contacted or affected, FTC consumer advice directs people to report fraud and to report identity theft when personal information or identity details may be involved. The same resource page also directs consumers to get a free credit report. These actions are available to consumers, but reporting is not itself a guarantee of a refund or recovery. [S2]

If you already sent money or information, keep the original records and separate the response by category. For personal information or possible identity theft, use the FTC’s identity-theft reporting information and free credit-report resource. For unwanted calls or messages, retain the evidence instead of replying or forwarding money. [S2]

## A simple scam stop checklist

Use this as a last check before any action:

1. **Pause:** End the call or stop the message and do not follow any new link or phone number. [S2]
2. **Verify:** Open the organization’s official website yourself or use an independently known contact channel. [S2]
3. **Decline:** Do not transfer money, withdraw cash, buy gold or disclose account credentials on request. [S1][S2]
4. **Document:** Save communications and payment details exactly as received. [S1]
5. **Report:** Use the FTC’s “Report fraud” or “Report identity theft” options when applicable. [S2]

The strongest consumer check is direct verification of the request, the recipient and the payment method before money or sensitive information leaves your control. The FTC’s enforcement actions show the scale of the agency’s response, but individual risk is controlled by the consumer’s next decision, not by the name on a message. [S1]
