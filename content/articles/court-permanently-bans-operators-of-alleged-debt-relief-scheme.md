A federal court has permanently banned the operators of a debt relief operation that the Federal Trade Commission said targeted seniors and veterans while impersonating banks, credit card companies and government agencies. The court also entered a $106.8 million default judgment against corporate defendants and ordered defendants to turn over assets, according to the FTC’s October 22 announcement. [S1]

The announcement is significant, but its limits matter. The FTC describes the conduct as an alleged scheme, and the judgment was entered by default. The excerpt provided does not explain what consumers must do, whether any money will be returned, or how a person could qualify for a payment. A judgment amount is not a promise that consumers will receive that amount—or any particular share of it. [S1]

## What changed

At the FTC’s request, a federal court permanently banned the operation’s operators. The court entered the default judgment on October 14, 2026, against corporate defendants, including Accelerated Debt Settlement Inc., ADS Resolve LLC and Financial Solutions Group LLC. The FTC says the judgment totaled $106.8 million and the court ordered defendants to turn over assets. [S1]

The FTC says the operation targeted seniors and veterans and that its operators impersonated banks, credit card companies and government agencies. Those are the agency’s allegations as described in the announcement; the supplied excerpt does not provide the complaint, the court’s reasoning, or more detail about the alleged tactics. [S1]

The practical takeaway is narrower than “the court will repay victims.” The announcement reports a ban, a default judgment and an asset-turnover order. It does not announce a consumer refund program, identify a claims process, give a deadline to apply, or say how much money may be available for consumers. Don’t treat the judgment figure as a personal refund estimate. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a company or caller says it can reduce or erase your debt, pause before paying or sharing personal information—especially if it claims to represent your bank, card issuer or a government agency. This case is a reminder to verify who is contacting you rather than relying on an official-sounding name or a caller’s claim of authority. The FTC describes the alleged impersonation in this case; the supplied announcement does not establish that every debt relief company is part of the operation. [S1]

Use contact details you already trust to reach the bank, card issuer or agency the person claims to represent. Ask whether it contacted you and whether the offer or request is genuine. Don’t rely on a phone number, link or payment instructions supplied in an unexpected call, message or email. These are verification steps, not a determination that a particular contact is connected to this case.

If you already paid or shared information, write down what happened, when it happened, how you were contacted, what you paid and any business name or account details you have. Contact the financial institution involved through a trusted channel and explain what happened. The FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud and links to identity-theft help and free credit reports; use its official website rather than a link sent by the person you’re concerned about. [S2]

Do not pay anyone who says a fee will guarantee recovery of money from this case. The FTC announcement supplied here does not describe an authorized claims administrator, a payment schedule or a consumer application process. Before responding to a supposed refund offer, check the FTC’s official case information and confirm that the process is real; the announcement alone is not evidence that an offer is legitimate. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

- **The contact’s identity:** Did your bank, card issuer or government agency actually contact you? Reach it through a number or website you obtained independently.
- **The offer’s connection to this case:** Does an official FTC notice identify a claims process or administrator? The announcement provided here does not. [S1]
- **Any payment request:** Is someone asking for an upfront fee, account access or sensitive information to secure a refund? Don’t assume the request is legitimate just because the caller mentions the court or FTC.
- **Your next step if you suspect fraud:** Keep relevant records, contact the financial institution involved through a trusted channel, and consider reporting the matter through the FTC’s official consumer site. [S2]

The court action may stop the named operators from continuing the operation, but the available announcement does not answer whether a consumer will recover money or what further steps a consumer should take to seek it. Verify any claim of a refund or official affiliation independently, and do not let pressure or a deadline supplied by an unsolicited contact rush your decision. [S1]
