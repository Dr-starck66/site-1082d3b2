A payment processor’s role can be easy to miss when a purchase goes wrong. In a September 4 announcement, the Federal Trade Commission said global processor Nuvei would pay $4.85 million to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud, including by opening and maintaining accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception. The FTC said the alleged schemes included tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

The settlement puts responsibility for screening merchants in the spotlight, but it does not establish that any particular Nuvei-processed transaction is fraudulent or tell consumers they will receive money. The practical takeaway: check the transaction and the seller before drawing conclusions, and report suspected fraud through the FTC’s fraud-reporting channel. [S1][S2]

## What changed

The FTC announced that Nuvei agreed to settle its charges and must implement robust merchant screening practices, as described in the agency’s headline. The proposed settlement amount is $4.85 million. The agency’s complaint alleged that Nuvei and its subsidiaries knowingly processed payments for merchants engaged in fraudulent activity, including tech support scams. Those are allegations resolved by settlement, not findings about every merchant or transaction handled by the company. [S1]

The central issue is the processor’s relationship with merchants: the FTC alleged that Nuvei opened and maintained processing accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception. That makes merchant screening—not simply the presence of a payment processor in a transaction—the consumer-protection focus of the announcement. [S1]

The supplied FTC announcement does not say that the $4.85 million will be distributed to consumers, identify a refund program, or set out a process for individuals to claim money. Consumers should not treat the settlement amount as an available refund pool or assume that they qualify for payment. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a charge seems suspicious, begin with the transaction itself. Compare the amount and date with receipts, order confirmations, subscriptions, and any recent contact with a seller. Write down the merchant name as it appears on your records and what you believe you paid for. This can help you separate a charge you recognize from one you do not; it does not, by itself, prove fraud.

Next, check whether the concern is about the purchase or the way it appears on an account. If you recognize the seller but did not receive what you expected, gather the order details and communications with that seller. If you do not recognize the transaction or believe someone deceived you into paying, keep the relevant records and consider reporting the suspected fraud to the FTC, which provides a “Report Fraud” option. [S2]

Do not infer that a charge is fraudulent just because you find a connection to Nuvei. The FTC announcement concerns allegations about processing accounts for particular merchants; it does not identify every Nuvei customer or say that all transactions processed by the company were improper. A processor’s name alone is not enough to determine what happened in an individual case. [S1]

Similarly, do not assume that the settlement automatically changes what you can recover on a particular purchase. The supplied announcement reports the settlement amount and the screening requirement, but it provides no individual eligibility criteria, application deadline, or instructions for obtaining consumer compensation. Check official FTC information before responding to messages that promise settlement money or ask you to pay a fee to claim it. [S1][S2]

A useful decision path is: if you can identify the purchase, review the seller and your records; if the transaction remains unexplained or appears deceptive, document why and report it to the FTC; if you are looking for settlement compensation, first verify that an official refund program and instructions actually exist. The evidence supplied here confirms no such consumer program. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Confirm the amount, date, merchant name, and what you believe you purchased. Keep receipts, order confirmations, and relevant messages together.
- **The claim:** Distinguish a charge you do not recognize from a dispute about a purchase you do recognize. Neither situation, on its own, confirms that the merchant committed fraud.
- **The processor connection:** Do not treat a Nuvei reference as proof that your specific transaction was among the conduct alleged by the FTC. The announcement does not identify individual consumer transactions. [S1]
- **Any promised payment:** Look for an official FTC announcement with eligibility rules and claim instructions before sharing personal or payment information. The supplied announcement does not describe a refund program for consumers. [S1]
- **Where to report:** The FTC’s consumer site lists a Report Fraud option. Use the official agency site rather than an unsolicited message claiming to represent the FTC. [S2]

Nuvei’s settlement is a reminder to pay attention to who is selling a service and to preserve a clear record when a payment seems wrong. But the evidence here supports a specific conclusion about the FTC’s charges and the company’s settlement—not a conclusion about any one consumer’s transaction or right to a refund. [S1]
