## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and taken enforcement actions that resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency highlighted those efforts in an October 29, 2026, update on scams in which fraudsters pose as trusted organizations or officials. [S1]

The figures describe agency actions, not a payment program open to everyone who has encountered an impostor. The announcement does not say that every consumer who lost money will receive compensation, or provide details in the supplied information about who qualified for redress in those enforcement cases. [S1]

The FTC also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That broad context is a reason to take suspicious approaches seriously, but it does not establish how likely any particular call, message, or website is to be fraudulent. [S1]

Impersonators may claim to represent the government, a business, or a charity. The FTC’s October 26 consumer alert describes scammers posing as charities and making people think they have won a sweepstakes sponsored by an organization they support. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Treat a demand to move or hand over money as a stop sign—not as proof that the person contacting you is legitimate. The FTC says it will never threaten you, tell you that you must transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. [S2]

A useful decision path is to pause, identify what the contact is asking you to do, and compare it with those warnings. If someone claiming to be with the FTC demands a transfer, cash withdrawal, or gold handoff, do not follow that instruction. The FTC identifies those demands as signs of a scam. [S2]

If the approach involves a prize, ask yourself whether you entered or otherwise have a reason to expect it—and whether the caller is using your past support for a charity to make the offer sound familiar. The FTC warns that scammers may impersonate a charity and suggest a person has won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. That warning does not establish whether any particular prize is real; it is a reason to verify before responding or sharing information. [S2]

If the contact does not match the FTC’s specific warnings, that alone is not a guarantee that it is safe. The supplied FTC materials describe examples of impersonation and an agency response, not a complete list of every scam tactic. Don’t treat an official-sounding name, a familiar charity, or the FTC’s enforcement figures as a substitute for checking the specific request. [S1][S2]

The $80 million figure should also be read narrowly: it is the amount of consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions cited by the FTC, not a promise of repayment to a person who reports a scam. The information supplied does not spell out the terms of those cases or an individual consumer’s eligibility. [S1]

If you believe someone is impersonating the FTC or another organization, the FTC’s consumer site offers a way to report fraud. Reporting is an escalation option; the supplied information does not promise that a report will recover money or lead to a particular enforcement result. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to an unexpected demand or prize notification, check:

- **What are they asking for?** A threat, a demand to transfer money to “protect it,” or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over match warnings the FTC gives about imposters. [S2]
- **What is the claimed connection?** If a caller says a charity is behind a prize, consider whether the offer relies on your support for that charity; the FTC specifically warns about this impersonation tactic. [S2]
- **What does the FTC’s announcement actually promise?** It reports 19 FTC-imposter websites removed and more than $80 million in redress from enforcement actions since the rule took effect in 2024; it does not say that every consumer can claim a share. [S1]
- **Where can you escalate?** The FTC’s consumer advice site lists reporting fraud as an action consumers can take. The materials provided do not describe a recovery guarantee or a case-specific reporting process. [S1][S2]
