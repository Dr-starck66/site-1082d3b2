The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The update is a reminder that scammers may borrow the identity of trusted institutions—and that enforcement against them does not, by itself, tell you whether you can recover money you lost. [S1]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, update, the FTC highlighted its ongoing efforts to combat impersonation scams. The agency said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

The FTC’s figures describe agency action: 19 FTC-imposter websites stopped and more than $80 million in consumer redress secured through enforcement actions since the rule went into effect in 2024. They do not say that every person who reports a scam will receive money, or that the reported redress is available to every scam victim. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer advice also draws a clear line around people claiming to represent the agency: it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. The agency says those demands are a scam. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Treat an unexpected demand for money from someone claiming to be with the FTC as a reason to stop, not as proof that you are in trouble or need to act immediately. A caller, text, email, or website may look official, but the FTC’s warning is about the demand itself: threats and instructions to move money or hand over cash or gold do not come from the agency. [S2]

A practical decision path:

- **If the message threatens you or demands a transfer, cash, or gold:** Don’t follow those instructions. The FTC says it will not make those demands. [S2]
- **If a person or site claims to be the FTC:** Don’t rely on its appearance or its claim. The agency’s report of 19 FTC-imposter websites shows that fake sites are among the impersonation tactics it has acted against. [S1]
- **If you’re unsure:** Pause before responding or sharing information. Use the FTC’s consumer advice site or fraud-reporting option to check guidance and report suspected fraud. [S2]
- **If you already paid or shared information:** The FTC lists options to report fraud and identity theft, and its consumer advice includes an article on what to do after a scam. Check those resources for steps that fit what happened. [S2]

The enforcement numbers are a sign that the agency is pursuing impersonators, not a substitute for checking your own situation. “Consumer redress” in the announcement refers to what the FTC says it secured through enforcement actions; the supplied update does not describe who qualifies for any particular redress or how to apply. Don’t treat the headline amount as a promise of repayment. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before doing anything in response to a supposed government warning, check the request against the FTC’s stated warning signs. Does it threaten you? Tell you to transfer money to protect it? Ask you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over? The FTC says it will never do those things. [S2]

Next, separate a claim about enforcement from a claim about your personal case. A message saying you are owed money, must pay a fee to receive it, or need to provide details to release a payout is not verified by the FTC’s announcement about more than $80 million in redress. The supplied announcement does not provide eligibility rules or a redress application process. [S1]

If you’re still uncertain, don’t use contact details or links supplied by the person making the demand as your only check. Go to the FTC’s consumer advice site yourself and use its fraud or identity-theft reporting options as appropriate. For a suspected scam, reporting it is an escalation option; it is not a guarantee of a refund or other outcome. [S2]

The central takeaway is simple: impersonators can imitate an agency, but they cannot turn an urgent demand into a legitimate one. Stop when you encounter the warning signs, verify through the FTC’s own consumer resources, and report suspected fraud rather than sending money or valuables. [S2]
