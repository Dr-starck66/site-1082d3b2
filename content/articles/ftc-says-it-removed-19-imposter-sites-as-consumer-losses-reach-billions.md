## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also says imposter scams have been the most-reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

The announcement is a reminder that impersonation can intersect with personal finances in more than one way: a message might falsely claim to come from a government agency, or a caller might claim to be collecting a debt. The FTC’s figures describe agency-wide efforts and reported losses; they do not establish that a particular message is genuine or that a specific consumer will receive money. [S1][S3]

## What this means for consumers

If a message says you owe money, separate two questions: who is contacting you, and whether the debt is yours. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, how to dispute the debt, and other information. If the collector will not or cannot provide it, the CFPB says the contact could be a scam. [S3]

A practical decision path:

- **First, pause on the claim.** Don’t treat a logo, official-sounding name, or urgent demand as proof of identity. The FTC’s announcement confirms that impersonators have used websites posing as the agency, but it does not describe a single verification method that works for every contact. [S1]
- **If the contact is about a debt, ask for the required details.** Write down the collector’s name, the creditor, the amount, and how to dispute the debt. Compare the information with your own records before deciding what to do. The CFPB advises using contact with a debt collector to find out about the debt and determine whether you owe it. [S3]
- **If the information is missing or the debt seems unfamiliar, don’t assume it is valid.** The CFPB says that a collector’s inability or refusal to provide the required information could indicate a scam. Its debt-collection resources include guidance on how to reply and how to seek proof of a debt. [S3]
- **If you’re concerned about your credit, review your reports.** The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year for errors that could lower a score or affect access to credit or loan terms. It says requesting your reports will not hurt your credit score and provides a list of reporting companies to contact. [S2]

Checking a credit report can help spot an unfamiliar entry, but it does not by itself prove who contacted you or settle whether a debt is yours. Keep those questions separate: ask the collector for debt information, and use your reports to look for credit-file problems. The CFPB provides separate resources for disputing a credit-report error and responding to a debt collector. [S2][S3]

The FTC’s reported $80 million-plus in redress is also not a reason to pay a fee or share sensitive information with someone promising to get you money. The announcement describes redress secured through agency enforcement actions; it does not say that every scam victim qualifies for a payment. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, disputing, or sharing personal or financial information, verify the specific claim:

- **The contact:** Does the person or site identify itself clearly, and can you confirm it independently? The FTC says it removed 19 websites impersonating the agency, so a site’s appearance alone should not settle the question. [S1]
- **The debt:** Can the collector provide the creditor’s name, amount owed, and information on how to dispute? If not, the CFPB says the contact could be a scam. [S3]
- **Your credit file:** Is there an unfamiliar item or an error? The CFPB recommends regular report checks and provides guidance on correcting errors. [S2]
- **The promised outcome:** Is someone implying the FTC’s enforcement results mean you personally will receive redress? The agency’s announcement reports an aggregate amount secured through enforcement, not an individual eligibility decision. [S1]

If a debt contact remains unclear, use the CFPB’s debt-collection resources to learn how to reply, seek proof, or understand complaint options; the bureau also lists a phone line for people who still need help. For a suspected fraud, the FTC’s site provides a way to report fraud. Keep records of the contact and the information you receive so you can explain what happened when seeking help. [S1][S3]
