## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The FTC announced those figures on October 29, 2026, as part of an update on efforts to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The agency also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. The announcement describes enforcement and website removals, but does not say that every person contacted by an impostor is eligible for money or that a consumer will receive a payment. [S1]

That distinction matters: an enforcement result is not the same as a personal claim or a guarantee of recovery. The supplied FTC announcement gives a combined redress figure, but does not identify individual eligibility rules, application steps, or a deadline for consumers to act. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a message or website claims to represent the FTC, treat the claim as something to verify—not proof that the contact is genuine. The FTC page notes that official federal government websites often use .gov or .mil addresses and that HTTPS indicates an encrypted connection. Those checks can help assess a website address, but they do not establish that a separate call, email, text, or payment request is authentic. [S1]

A practical decision path is to first identify what the contact wants. If the person is discussing a debt, switch to the CFPB’s debt-collection guidance rather than relying on a caller’s description. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information about how to dispute the debt, and other information; if the collector will not or cannot provide it, the contact could be a scam. [S3]

If you do not recognize the debt, the CFPB advises using the contact to find out about it and determine whether you owe it. Its guidance also says to tell the collector if you believe the debt is not yours. Do not treat a demand as verified merely because it sounds official; use the information the collector provides to assess the debt before deciding what to do. [S3]

If the contact instead concerns your credit or you suspect your identity or credit information may be involved, review your credit reports for unfamiliar or incorrect information. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year, says requesting them will not hurt your credit score, and notes that its resources explain how to dispute errors. [S2]

A report review is a way to look for problems, not proof by itself that an impostor caused them. The CFPB says checking reports can help spot issues early and points consumers to a list of credit reporting companies to request and review reports. If you find an error, follow the CFPB’s dispute guidance rather than assuming that a suspicious contact has already been resolved. [S2]

Keep the response proportional to what you can verify. If there is a debt, seek the required debt information and assess whether it is yours. If there is a possible credit-report problem, inspect the reports and use the dispute process for errors. If someone claims you are owed money because of an FTC case, look for the specific case and instructions on an official FTC source; the agency-wide amount announced in October is not an individual award notice. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, sharing personal information, or responding to a claim about a debt or refund, check:

- **Who is contacting you:** Compare the website address with the FTC’s official government site. A .gov or .mil address and HTTPS are useful website checks, but they do not authenticate a separate message or caller. [S1]
- **What the debt is:** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount, and information on how to dispute it. If the collector cannot or will not provide the information, the CFPB says the contact could be a scam. [S3]
- **Whether your credit file shows a problem:** Request and review your credit reports; checking them does not hurt your credit score. Use the CFPB’s dispute resources if you find an error. [S2]
- **Whether a promised payment is actually meant for you:** The FTC’s reported redress total does not establish your eligibility. Verify any case-specific notice and instructions through an official source before acting. [S1]

The evidence supplied here does not provide an FTC reporting form, a redress application deadline, or a procedure for an individual to claim part of the announced total. Do not infer those details from a headline or an unsolicited message; verify any next step through the relevant agency’s official resources. [S1]
