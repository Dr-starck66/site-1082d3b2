The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and obtained more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement underscores a practical point for consumers: a message that looks official is not proof that the person contacting you represents the government. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer guidance offers a simple test for one particularly alarming kind of demand: the agency will not threaten you, tell you to move money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. If a caller or message makes one of those demands while claiming to be the FTC, treat it as a scam warning sign, not an instruction to follow. [S2]

## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the FTC highlighted its continuing work against impersonation scams. The agency said its efforts included taking down 19 FTC-imposter websites and enforcement actions that resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. Those are reported agency-wide results, not a promise that a particular consumer will receive money. [S1]

The announcement also described the scale of the problem: the FTC said imposter scams had been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That describes reports and reported losses; it does not establish that every attempted scam is reported or that every report has been independently verified. [S1]

The FTC’s separate consumer advice makes the risk concrete. Scammers may impersonate the FTC and use threats or a supposed need to “protect” a person’s money to push them toward handing over funds. The agency says it will not make those demands. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Think of the FTC’s warning as a decision point, not a test of whether a caller sounds convincing. If someone claiming to represent the agency threatens you, urges you to transfer money for safekeeping, or tells you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to another person, do not follow that instruction. The FTC identifies those demands as signs of a scam. [S2]

Next, separate the claim from the requested action. A person may use an agency’s name, a familiar logo, or official-sounding language; that does not make a demand genuine. The FTC’s announcement about websites impersonating the agency is a reminder that a convincing online appearance can also be faked. [S1][S2]

If the contact does not include those specific demands, do not treat that alone as proof that it is legitimate. Instead, pause before sending money or personal information, and assess what the person is asking you to do. The evidence provided here supports a clear warning about threats and instructions to move or hand over money; it does not provide a complete test for authenticating every call, email, text, or website. [S2]

Keep the enforcement figure in perspective, too. More than $80 million in consumer redress reflects the results of FTC enforcement actions since the rule took effect; it is not evidence that a person who reports a scam will automatically qualify for a payment. Do not rely on a caller’s promise of a refund or payout as a reason to give them money or information. [S1]

A practical response is to stop the interaction when a warning sign appears, avoid acting under pressure, and use the FTC’s consumer site to report fraud. If you are unsure whether a message is genuine, the source material does not offer a universal verification procedure, so avoid treating the contact itself as authoritative. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before taking any step prompted by a supposed FTC contact, check:

- **Is there a threat?** The FTC says it will not threaten you. A threat tied to an urgent payment demand is a reason to stop, not comply. [S2]
- **Are you being told to move money?** The FTC says it will not tell you to transfer money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S2]
- **Is the person promising redress?** The reported $80 million-plus is a combined enforcement result since 2024, not a guarantee of an individual refund. [S1]
- **What do you know about the contact?** The supplied FTC guidance identifies specific scam demands but does not confirm that an unthreatening message or official-looking website is real. Do not use appearance alone as verification. [S1][S2]

If you suspect fraud, the FTC’s consumer advice page provides a route to report it. Reporting is an escalation option; the supplied information does not promise a particular investigation, recovery, or outcome for an individual report. [S2]
