## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission announced on September 4, 2026, that global payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The agency says Nuvei and its subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

The case focuses on the processor behind merchants’ payments, not only on the businesses consumers may have seen or contacted. The FTC’s allegation is that Nuvei enabled merchants accused of deception to process payments; the settlement amount is described as a payment to settle the FTC’s charges. The supplied announcement does not say that consumers will receive money from this settlement. [S1]

That distinction matters if you recognize a suspicious charge. A regulator’s settlement with a payment processor does not, by itself, identify every merchant involved or say what will happen with a particular transaction. The FTC announcement provided here names tech support scams as an example, but does not list merchants, customer accounts, or individual transactions. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a payment seems connected to a tech-support offer or another purchase you now doubt, start with the transaction itself: note the date, amount, and merchant name shown on your statement, and save any receipt, emails, texts, or website details you still have. These records can help you describe what happened when you contact the business or seek guidance. The FTC says consumers can report fraud through its website, but the supplied materials do not promise that a report will recover money. [S1][S2]

Don’t assume that seeing “Nuvei” in a payment record means Nuvei was the seller, or that the settlement automatically covers your purchase. The FTC describes Nuvei as a payment processor and says its case involved processing accounts for merchants; it does not provide a consumer-facing list to match against a statement. Verify who sold the product or service and what the charge was for before drawing conclusions. [S1]

If you believe the seller misrepresented a service or charged you for something you did not agree to, contact the seller using contact details you can independently verify and keep a record of the response. You can also consult the FTC’s consumer advice resources or report suspected fraud. Treat the report as a way to share information with the agency—not as confirmation that the FTC has identified your specific seller or will issue you a refund. [S2]

For a charge you do not recognize, use the customer-service contact information on your card or account statement to ask the financial institution what steps are available for that transaction. Describe what you know, rather than assuming the FTC settlement determines the outcome. The supplied sources do not explain card-dispute deadlines, eligibility, or required procedures, so check directly with your institution for its instructions. [S1]

Be wary of anyone who contacts you claiming they can release money from the Nuvei settlement in exchange for a fee or personal information. The FTC’s general consumer site provides a way to report fraud and warns that government impersonation is a consumer-scam topic; the supplied Nuvei announcement does not describe a direct consumer payout program. Verify any claimed payment through official FTC information instead of relying on an unsolicited message. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Use this decision path before sending more money or sharing account details:

- **Match the transaction.** Compare the amount and date with your own records. Identify the merchant and the product or service you expected to receive. Don’t treat a processor name as proof of who made the sale.
- **Check the claim.** If someone says you qualify for money because of the Nuvei case, look for confirmation on an official FTC page. The announcement supplied here describes a $4.85 million settlement with the FTC, not a process for consumers to claim a share. [S1]
- **Contact the right parties.** Reach out to the seller through contact information you can verify. If the transaction is unfamiliar or disputed, ask your card issuer or financial institution what options and time limits apply; the provided sources do not state those rules. [S1]
- **Keep a paper trail.** Save transaction details, communications, and any explanation you receive. If you suspect fraud, the FTC’s consumer site offers a fraud-reporting route. [S2]
- **Pause before paying a supposed recovery service.** Verify the organization and the claimed settlement process independently; do not rely on an unsolicited promise of a refund.

The central takeaway is narrow but useful: the FTC says Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to resolve charges tied to merchant screening and alleged processing for deceptive businesses. That is a significant enforcement action, but the evidence provided does not establish that any particular consumer is eligible for payment, or that a charge on a statement was part of the case. Verify the merchant, document the transaction, and ask the relevant institution or the FTC for guidance before acting. [S1][S2]
