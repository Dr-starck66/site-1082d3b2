The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

That update is a reminder to treat unexpected contact claiming to come from the FTC with care. It is not a notice that every person who lost money to an imposter will receive a payment, or that a particular website or message is legitimate. The FTC’s release describes agency-wide actions, not a process for identifying individual consumers who may qualify for redress. [S1]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, announcement, the FTC highlighted its efforts to combat impersonation scams. The stated results include stopping 19 websites that impersonated the FTC and enforcement actions resulting in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

Those figures describe the FTC’s actions; the release excerpt does not name the websites, list the enforcement cases, explain how the redress total is divided, or say whether the agency is contacting consumers about payments. The numbers are useful context about enforcement, but they are not enough to verify a message claiming to offer money or to establish that a specific consumer is eligible. [S1]

The FTC also points to the scale of the problem: it says imposter scams have ranked as the top reported fraud category for several years and that consumers have reported billions in losses. That is a statement about reports and reported losses, not a count of every scam or a prediction about how much any one person might lose. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Start with the request, not the caller’s claimed title. The FTC’s consumer advice says the agency will never threaten you, say you must transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. If a person claiming to represent the FTC makes one of those demands, treat it as a scam warning and do not follow the payment instructions. [S2]

A practical decision path:

- **Is the contact asking you to move money, withdraw cash, or hand over gold?** Stop before paying or transferring anything. Those demands contradict the FTC’s stated warning about how it will contact consumers. [S2]
- **Is the contact only claiming to be official, without making one of those demands?** Don’t assume the claim is genuine based on a name, logo, or website appearance. The FTC’s announcement confirms that websites have impersonated the agency, but does not provide a complete test for authenticating every call, email, text, or site. [S1][S2]
- **Are you on a website before sharing sensitive information?** The FTC advises checking that it is an official federal government website; it says official government websites usually end in .gov or .mil and that https indicates a secure connection. Those checks apply to a website connection and do not, on their own, prove that a message or person who sent you there is legitimate. [S2]
- **Have you already paid or shared information?** The FTC’s consumer site provides options to report fraud and identity theft, and links to advice on what to do if you were scammed. Use those official resources to identify next steps for your situation rather than relying on instructions from the person who contacted you. [S2]

Reporting and enforcement serve different purposes. The FTC’s announcement describes agency action against imposter websites and enforcement cases; its consumer site separately offers a way to report fraud. The supplied information does not promise that filing a report will recover money, trigger a refund, or lead to a particular legal outcome. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message that claims to involve the FTC, check these points:

- **What exactly is being requested?** A threat, a demand to transfer money to “protect it,” or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over are specific FTC-identified warning signs. Don’t comply with those instructions. [S2]
- **Where did the link take you?** Check the address yourself for an official federal domain and a secure connection before sharing sensitive information. A familiar-looking page is not enough; the FTC says imposter websites have been stopped. [S1][S2]
- **Is someone promising redress?** The $80 million figure is the reported total resulting from enforcement actions since the rule took effect. The announcement excerpt does not identify individual recipients or confirm that you are owed money. Verify any claim through FTC resources you reach independently, not through contact details supplied in an unsolicited message. [S1][S2]
- **What happened to your money or information?** The FTC site separates fraud reporting from identity-theft reporting and provides an article for people who paid a suspected scammer or shared personal information or device access. Choose the resource that matches what happened, and consult the linked guidance for details not covered in the agency announcement. [S2]

The safest next step depends on the contact and what, if anything, you shared. The FTC’s headline figures show enforcement activity, while its consumer advice offers a clear boundary: do not send money or valuables in response to a supposed FTC demand to protect your funds. [S1][S2]
