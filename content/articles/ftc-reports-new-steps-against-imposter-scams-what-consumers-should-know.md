## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency described these as highlights of its ongoing effort to combat impersonation scams. The announcement does not say that every consumer who encounters an impersonator is eligible for money, or that the total represents a new payment available to the public. [S1]

The FTC also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That is a report of what consumers have said they lost; it is not a forecast of future losses or a measure of how much any particular person will recover. [S1]

A recent FTC consumer alert illustrates how impersonation can target people through a familiar organization, not just a government agency. The alert warns that scammers sometimes pose as reputable charities and may tell someone who donated to a charity that they won a sweepstakes sponsored by it. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

The practical takeaway is to judge a request by what the person is asking you to do—not by how official the caller, message, website, or claimed organization looks. The FTC says it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. If a person claiming to represent the FTC makes one of those demands, treat it as a scam and do not follow the instructions. [S2]

Use a pause-and-check decision path:

- **If the message claims to be from the FTC:** Stop before sharing information or moving money. Compare the request with the FTC’s stated warning: it will not threaten you or direct you to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold to hand over. A demand like that is a clear reason not to proceed. [S2]
- **If a charity or sweepstakes is involved:** Be wary of an unexpected call saying you won a prize because you donated. Do not rely on the caller’s claim alone. Independently look up the charity’s contact details and ask whether it recognizes the sweepstakes and the person contacting you. The FTC’s alert specifically flags this kind of charity impersonation and supposed prize claim. [S2]
- **If the request does not fit those examples:** Do not treat the absence of a particular warning sign as proof that the contact is legitimate. Pause and verify the organization through contact information you find independently, rather than details supplied in the suspicious message. This is a practical way to check a claim without relying on the impersonator’s own channel. [S1][S2]

The FTC’s announcement is evidence of enforcement activity, not an assurance that scams have stopped. The 19 removed websites were described as sites impersonating the FTC; the release does not establish that every suspicious site has been found or removed. Likewise, the more than $80 million figure refers to consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions since the rule took effect. It should not be read as an automatic refund, a promise of recovery, or an offer to pay anyone who reports a scam. [S1]

If you have already interacted with a suspected impersonator, stop following any further instructions and use the FTC’s fraud-reporting option listed on its consumer site. If someone is claiming you are entitled to money from an FTC action, verify that claim through official FTC information rather than paying a fee or giving personal details in response to an unsolicited message. The supplied FTC materials identify a report-fraud service and FTC refund programs, but do not describe the eligibility or process for any particular case. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding, paying, or sharing information, check:

- **Who initiated contact:** Did the person reach you unexpectedly, and can you confirm the organization using contact details you found independently? [S2]
- **What they want you to do:** A threat, a demand to transfer money for protection, or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over do not match what the FTC says it does. [S2]
- **Whether a prize claim checks out:** If a caller says you won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes, verify the claim directly with the charity before acting. [S2]
- **Whether a payment claim is real:** The FTC’s announcement of redress does not establish that you personally qualify. Check official FTC information for the specific case, and do not assume that a message promising a payout is genuine. [S1]
- **Where to report:** The FTC’s consumer site lists an option to report fraud. Use the official site rather than a link supplied by someone whose identity you have not verified. [S2]
