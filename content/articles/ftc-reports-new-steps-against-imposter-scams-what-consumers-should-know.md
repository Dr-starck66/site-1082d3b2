## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency described those results as part of ongoing efforts to fight impersonation scams. The figure is an aggregate enforcement result, not a promise that any particular person will receive money. [S1]

The FTC also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That is a warning about the scale of the problem, not a measure of how likely any one call or message is to be fraudulent. [S1]

The agency’s practical message is especially relevant when someone claims to be from the FTC: the FTC says it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Treat a demand to move or hand over money as a stop sign, not as proof that the person contacting you is an official. If a caller or message claims you must transfer funds for safety, withdraw cash, or buy gold for someone, do not follow that instruction. Those are actions the FTC specifically says it will not demand. [S2]

A useful decision path is to focus on what the person wants you to do, rather than how convincing their title, logo, or story sounds. If the demand matches one of the FTC’s warnings, end the transaction: do not send the money or give it to the person. If the contact does not include those demands, that alone does not establish that it is genuine; the supplied FTC guidance identifies specific red flags, not a complete test for every possible scam. [S2]

For example, imagine a message claiming to be from the FTC that says your money is at risk and you must move it immediately. The key issue is not whether the message looks official or uses government language. The requested transfer conflicts with the FTC’s stated practice, so the safer choice is not to act on the message. [S2]

The same warning applies if someone says to take out cash or buy gold and deliver it to them. Changing the form of payment does not make the demand safer: the FTC explicitly includes both actions in its warning about imposters. [S2]

The FTC’s enforcement announcement is evidence of agency action, but it does not mean every fraudulent website has been removed or that a reported scam will lead to a payment to the person who reports it. The agency describes the removals and redress as results of its efforts; it does not say that consumers should pay fees, share financial details, or respond to unsolicited messages to claim redress. [S1]

If you believe you encountered fraud, the FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud and directs consumers to its advice on what to do if they were scammed. Reporting and consulting that advice are reasonable next steps; neither should be mistaken for a guarantee of recovering money. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message or giving anyone money, check the request against the FTC’s specific warning: is the person threatening you, asking you to transfer funds to protect them, or directing you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over? If so, do not proceed. [S2]

If the person says you are owed money because of an FTC case, verify that claim through FTC information you seek out yourself rather than relying on the message making the claim. The announcement reports more than $80 million in consumer redress across enforcement actions, but the supplied information gives no individual eligibility rules, claim deadline, or method for determining whether a particular person qualifies. Do not assume the headline amount applies to you. [S1]

If you have already acted on a suspicious demand, use the FTC’s “What To Do if You Were Scammed” consumer advice and fraud-reporting options as starting points. The FTC lists both resources, but the available information here does not specify a recovery process or outcome for every situation. [S2]
