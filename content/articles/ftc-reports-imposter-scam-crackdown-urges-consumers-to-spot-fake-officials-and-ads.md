The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency describes the work as ongoing, against a backdrop of imposter scams that have been among the top reported fraud categories for several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

That announcement is a reminder that a convincing name, logo, or official-sounding message is not proof that the person contacting you represents a government agency, company, or charity. The FTC’s consumer advice highlights a particularly dangerous demand: someone claiming to be from the FTC who says to move money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over. The FTC says it will never make those demands. [S2]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, update, the FTC summarized its efforts to combat impersonation scams and protect consumers. The headline figures were 19 FTC-imposter websites removed and more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The agency did not say in the supplied announcement that the redress is a payment consumers can claim simply because they encountered a scam. [S1]

The FTC has also called attention to imposter ads online. Its consumer alert says scammers may pose as well-known businesses or government agencies in social media feeds, search results, and shopping platforms, hoping people will click. The agency says it is exploring additional ways to protect consumers from those ads and wants to hear from consumers. [S2]

Impersonation can involve charities, too. In a separate October 26 alert, the FTC warned that scammers may pose as reputable charities and tell donors they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. That example shows why a message can feel plausible—it may refer to a real organization or a past donation—without proving the sender is connected to it. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Treat a demand to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold for “protection” as a clear warning sign if the caller or message claims to be from the FTC. Don’t follow those instructions. This warning is specifically about what the FTC says it will never ask you to do; it is not a guarantee that every other kind of request is legitimate. [S2]

If an ad or message claims to represent a business, government agency, or charity, separate the claim from the channel it arrived through. An ad appearing in a search result, social feed, or shopping platform is not, by itself, confirmation that the organization is behind it. As a cautious next step, pause before clicking, sharing personal information, or paying, and look for a way to confirm the offer or request directly with the organization. The FTC’s alert specifically warns that imposter ads use those online placements to attract clicks. [S2]

For a supposed charity sweepstakes, do not treat a reference to a charity you support as proof that you won. The FTC warns that scammers may use the connection between a donation and a claimed prize to make a false notice seem credible. If the message asks you to act, verify the claim before responding. [S2]

Reporting is another option: the FTC’s consumer pages provide a route to report fraud, and its imposter-ad alert invites consumers to tell the agency about imposter ads online. Reporting a scam or ad is not the same as applying for redress, and the supplied information does not promise that a report will lead to money being returned. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Use this quick decision path before responding to an unexpected call, message, or ad:

- **Is someone claiming to be from the FTC and demanding that you move or withdraw money, or buy and hand over gold?** Stop. The FTC says it will never make those demands. [S2]
- **Did you encounter the offer in an ad, search result, social feed, or shopping platform?** Don’t assume the placement proves who published it. Verify the claim with the named organization before clicking or paying. [S2]
- **Does a sweepstakes notice mention a charity you support?** Confirm that the charity is actually associated with the prize before sharing information or following instructions. The FTC warns that scammers use this kind of connection to make fake prizes believable. [S2]
- **Are you expecting money because the FTC announced more than $80 million in redress?** The announcement reports an enforcement result; it does not, in the supplied text, establish that you qualify for a payment. Check the FTC’s official refund-program information for any specific case before sharing details or relying on a payment claim. [S1]

The FTC’s figures describe agency enforcement, not a way to determine whether a particular caller, ad, or prize is genuine. For an immediate decision, focus on the request itself: a demand to hand over money or gold in the FTC’s name conflicts with the agency’s stated policy; other claims still need independent verification.
