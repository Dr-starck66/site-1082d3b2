A payment processor may be behind the scenes when a consumer pays a business, but the Federal Trade Commission says it can still matter when that business is accused of deception. The agency has reached a settlement with global processor Nuvei over allegations that it opened and maintained accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deceptive activity, including tech support scams. [S1]

The settlement calls for Nuvei to pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant-screening practices. That is an enforcement outcome involving the company; the FTC announcement does not say that consumers will receive a share of the payment or that an individual customer is due a refund. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC announced the settlement on September 4, 2026. Its complaint alleges that Nuvei Corporation and subsidiaries knowingly processed payments for merchants involved in fraud, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. These are allegations described by the FTC; the supplied announcement does not establish that every Nuvei-processed transaction, merchant, or customer was affected. [S1]

The announced terms include a $4.85 million payment and a requirement for Nuvei to strengthen its merchant screening. Those measures concern the processor’s practices. The announcement does not provide a consumer claim form, application deadline, refund schedule, or a promise of individual compensation. [S1]

That distinction matters if you recognize a business or charge connected to a suspected scam. A settlement payment to a government agency or under an enforcement action is not, by itself, proof that a particular customer qualifies for money. The available announcement does not identify affected consumers or explain a process for them to seek payment. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Treat the news as a reason to check a suspicious transaction—not as confirmation that you are owed money. Start with your receipt, account history, or payment statement. Write down the transaction date, amount, and merchant name as it appears there. Keep emails, messages, invoices, and any notes from calls or chats; this gives you a clearer record if you contact the business or a financial institution. The FTC announcement does not identify individual transactions or provide a way to match a charge to its allegations. [S1]

If you believe a merchant misled you, separate two questions: what happened with the purchase, and whether the charge is unfamiliar or incorrect. Describe the issue accurately when you contact the merchant or payment provider. Do not assume that Nuvei was the company you paid directly, or that the settlement changes the terms of your transaction; the announcement identifies Nuvei as a payment processor and does not explain how to resolve individual disputes. [S1]

If the concern involves an unexpected debt-collection call rather than a purchase charge, the CFPB says to use the contact to find out about the debt and determine whether you owe it. A collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt, among other information; if the collector cannot or will not provide it, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

If you suspect a problem may involve identity theft or affect your credit record, review your credit reports for information you do not recognize. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year and says requesting them will not hurt your credit score. If you find an error, the agency provides a guide to disputing it. [S2]

For an unresolved concern, the FTC’s announcement includes a link to report fraud, and the CFPB pages provide a consumer complaint option. Keep your records and state what you know, distinguishing a confirmed charge or contact from a suspicion. Reporting a concern does not guarantee a refund or a particular outcome. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Before sharing information or making a payment, pause and check:

- **The transaction:** Does the amount and date match your own records? Is the merchant name recognizable, even if it differs from the name you remember?
- **The request:** Is someone asking you to pay a debt, or are you dealing with a purchase you believe was deceptive? The next step depends on what happened; for a collection contact, ask for the creditor, amount, and dispute instructions. [S3]
- **The caller or message:** Do not treat a reference to Nuvei or the FTC as proof that the person contacting you is legitimate. Verify the claim using contact details you locate independently, and do not rely on a caller’s promise of settlement money. The FTC announcement does not describe a consumer payout program. [S1]
- **Your credit file:** If the issue may have affected your credit, check your reports and use the CFPB’s dispute guidance for an error. Requesting your reports does not hurt your score. [S2]
- **Your next contact:** Keep copies of records and note whom you contacted and when. If you remain unsure, use the FTC’s fraud-reporting route or the CFPB’s complaint option rather than assuming the settlement resolves your individual case. [S1][S2][S3]

The practical takeaway is limited but useful: the FTC says Nuvei must improve merchant screening, but the announcement does not give consumers a direct route to settlement money. Check the specific transaction or contact that concerns you, document it, and choose the agency or dispute channel that fits the problem. [S1][S2][S3]
