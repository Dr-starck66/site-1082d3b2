A call, text or letter about an unfamiliar debt can put pressure on you to pay quickly. But a collector’s first contact is also a chance to find out what the debt is and decide whether you owe it. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says a collector must provide information including the creditor’s name, the amount owed and how to dispute the debt; if the collector will not or cannot provide that information, it could be a scam. [S1]

The practical approach is to slow the conversation down, get the information in writing, compare it with your own records, and resolve any mismatch before sending money. Don’t treat a familiar company name—or an unfamiliar one—as proof either way. This article focuses on the checks supported by the information a collector is required to provide and the contact limits described by federal agencies. [S1][S2]

## Step-by-step: verify before you pay

### 1. Identify who is contacting you

Write down the collector’s name, the company it says it represents, and how it reached you. Ask for the name of the creditor connected to the debt. The CFPB identifies the creditor’s name as information a collector is required to provide. If that information is missing, or the collector will not provide it, treat the gap as a reason to pause and verify—not as a reason to pay just to end the conversation. [S1]

Do not assume that the name of the collection company is the name of the original creditor. Ask the collector to clarify which creditor it means. If you recognize the creditor but not the account, compare the details with your statements or other records before deciding the claim is yours. The goal is to establish what the collector says the debt relates to, not to accept a claim based on a familiar brand alone. [S1]

### 2. Get the amount and the dispute instructions

Ask for the amount the collector says is owed and how to dispute the debt. Those are among the details the CFPB says a collector must provide. Keep the notice or message, and make a note of what information is missing or unclear. A demand that gives you no usable way to identify or dispute the claim does not answer the basic verification questions. [S1]

Compare the amount with any records you have, such as account statements or payment confirmations. Look for a difference between what you remember owing and what the collector claims, or a payment you made that does not appear to have been counted. This comparison is a practical check, not a substitute for the collector’s required information. If the numbers do not match, ask for clarification before paying. [S1]

### 3. Decide whether the debt could be yours

Consider whether the creditor and account details match a debt you recognize. If they do not, say that you do not believe you owe the debt and ask how to dispute it. The CFPB advises consumers who believe they do not owe a debt to tell the collector; avoiding the collector is unlikely to make contact stop. [S1]

A name or account you do not recognize is a reason to investigate, not proof by itself that the claim is valid or fraudulent. The CFPB says collectors can help consumers understand whether a debt is theirs and what options they have, even if they cannot pay at the time. Use that conversation to get specific information rather than letting urgency take the place of checking. [S1]

### 4. Check for an obvious mismatch or missing detail

Use a short comparison before deciding what to do:

- **Creditor:** Does the creditor named by the collector match a debt you recognize? [S1]
- **Amount:** Does the stated amount match your records, or is there a difference you need explained? [S1]
- **Dispute route:** Has the collector explained how you can dispute the debt? [S1]
- **Your own records:** Do statements or payment records support the claim, contradict it or leave you unsure? Keep those records together while you seek clarification. [S1]

These checks can reveal questions to raise; they cannot, by themselves, prove that a debt is valid or invalid. If the collector’s information is incomplete, do not fill in the gaps by guessing. Ask for the required details and use the dispute instructions provided. The CFPB says missing information may be a sign of a scam, but does not say that every incomplete contact is fraudulent. [S1]

### 5. Choose the next step based on what you find

If the creditor, amount and account details make sense, you can consider your options without assuming you must pay immediately. The CFPB notes that a collector can discuss options even when you cannot pay at the time. If you still have questions, ask them before deciding whether to make a payment. [S1]

If you think the debt is not yours, or the amount or creditor information is wrong, tell the collector what you dispute and follow the instructions it provided. Keep a copy of your message and the collector’s response. The CFPB’s guidance is to tell the collector if you believe you do not owe the debt; it also points consumers to resources on disputing a debt. [S1]

If the collector cannot or will not provide the creditor’s name, amount or dispute information, stop short of paying while you check the claim. The CFPB says that failure could indicate a scam. You can use the CFPB’s consumer complaint channel if you need to raise an issue with a financial product or service. [S1]

## What a debt collector may contact you about

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) covers household debts such as credit card debt, car loans, medical bills, student loans and mortgages; the FTC says business debts are not covered by that law. If a collector is pursuing a business debt, do not assume that the FDCPA protections described here apply to it. [S2]

For debts covered by the FDCPA, the FTC says a collector generally cannot contact you before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. unless you agree, or call you at work if you tell the collector that calls there are not allowed. It also says a collector cannot call more than seven times within a seven-day period, or within seven days after speaking with you by phone about a particular debt. [S2]

The FTC also says you can ask a collector to stop contacting you by email or text, and ask it to stop private messages on social media. These limits concern contact methods and frequency; they do not verify whether a debt is yours. Keep the two questions separate: first check the claim, and also tell the collector if its contact is happening in a way the FTC describes as restricted. [S2]

## Common cases that call for a pause

**You recognize the creditor but not the debt.** Ask what account the collector means and compare the details with your records. A creditor name alone does not answer whether the specific claim is yours; the collector must provide the creditor’s name, amount and dispute information. [S1]

**The amount is unfamiliar.** Do not treat the figure as confirmed simply because it appears in a message or is repeated on a call. Ask for the amount owed and how to dispute the debt, then compare it with your records. If you still cannot reconcile it, explain what does not match and use the dispute instructions. [S1]

**The collector will not give basic details.** The CFPB says a collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, amount owed and how to dispute the debt. If the collector cannot or will not provide them, the CFPB says it could be a scam. Do not let a demand for quick payment replace the missing information. [S1]

**You cannot afford to pay now.** The CFPB says a collector can help explain what the debt is and what options you have even if you cannot pay at the time. You can focus first on identifying the debt and understanding the amount rather than making a payment you are not ready to make. [S1]

**You are tempted to ignore every contact.** The CFPB says ignoring or avoiding a collector is unlikely to make the collector stop contacting you. If you believe you do not owe the debt, tell the collector. A clear dispute or request for information is more useful than assuming silence will settle the question. [S1]

## A practical checklist before sending money

- Have you identified the collector and the creditor it says is connected to the debt? [S1]
- Do you know the amount the collector claims you owe? [S1]
- Has the collector explained how to dispute the debt? [S1]
- Have you compared the claim with account or payment records you already have? [S1]
- If something is wrong or unclear, have you told the collector what you dispute and kept a copy of your communication? [S1]
- If you are not ready or able to pay, have you asked what options are available rather than paying under pressure? [S1]

If you cannot answer one of the first three questions, you have not yet gathered the basic information the CFPB says the collector must provide. Pause and ask for it. If you can answer them but the claim still does not match your records, use the dispute instructions and make the disagreement specific. If the information checks out, consider your options at a pace that lets you understand what you are agreeing to. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, make sure you can identify the creditor, state the amount the collector claims and locate the collector’s instructions for disputing it. Compare those details with your own records, and flag anything you cannot explain. If the collector will not provide the required information, or you believe the debt is not yours, do not treat the demand alone as proof; ask for the details, tell the collector what you dispute, and consider submitting a complaint to the CFPB if you need help raising the issue. [S1]
