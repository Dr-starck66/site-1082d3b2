The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites that impersonated the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The update is a reminder that scammers can borrow a government agency’s name to make a demand feel urgent and official. It does not mean every person contacted by an impostor is eligible for money. [S1]

The FTC also warns consumers about a specific demand: someone claiming to be with the agency tells you to move money to “protect it,” withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over. The FTC says those are scam tactics, not instructions to follow. [S2]

## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the FTC highlighted its efforts to combat impersonation scams. The agency said it had removed 19 FTC-imposter websites and that enforcement actions had produced more than $80 million in consumer redress since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

That figure is an aggregate description of enforcement outcomes, not a promise of payment to anyone who has received a suspicious call, message, or website link. The announcement does not identify a new application process or say that people targeted by a scam automatically qualify for redress. Treat any unsolicited message claiming you have money waiting as something to verify independently. [S1]

The FTC described impostor scams as the top reported fraud category over several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That context explains why the agency is publicizing enforcement, but it does not establish how common any particular scam is in your community or whether a specific contact is genuine. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Use the demand itself as a decision point. If a person claiming to represent the FTC says you must transfer money to protect it, take out cash, or buy gold and give it to someone, stop. The FTC identifies those requests as a scam. Do not follow the instructions while trying to determine whether the caller sounds convincing. [S2]

Then separate the contact from any follow-up you choose to make. Don’t use a phone number, link, or website supplied by the person making the demand to check their identity. Instead, find the FTC’s official consumer website yourself and use its fraud-reporting option. The FTC’s site says official federal websites use .gov addresses and that https indicates a secure connection; those signs help check a site’s address and connection, but do not prove that an unexpected message is legitimate. [S1][S2]

A quick decision path can help when a message creates pressure:

- **Asked to move or hand over money?** Stop and do not comply; the FTC specifically flags those instructions as a scam. [S2]
- **Directed to a website?** Check the address rather than relying on a logo or official-sounding wording. FTC guidance identifies .gov as a mark of federal government websites and https as a secure connection. [S1]
- **Claimed you are owed money?** Don’t assume the FTC’s reported redress total applies to you. The announcement does not say that everyone contacted by an impostor receives money. [S1]
- **Want to alert the agency?** Use the FTC’s Report Fraud option from its consumer site rather than continuing through the suspicious contact. [S2]

The FTC update is about enforcement against impersonation and websites, not a guarantee that a scam will be stopped before it reaches you or that a reported loss will be recovered. The agency’s reported redress shows that enforcement actions have resulted in consumer redress, but the supplied announcement does not spell out who qualifies, how much any individual may receive, or how to claim it. Avoid paying a fee or sharing financial details based solely on a promise of FTC money. [S1]

If you have already responded, focus on what you actually did: whether you transferred funds, handed over cash or gold, or only received a message. The FTC offers a fraud-reporting route, but the information available here does not specify the next steps for every type of loss or personal-information exposure. Report what happened through the official channel and verify any further instructions independently. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before you respond to a message about an FTC case or payment, check the website address and whether the claim appears through an official FTC source you reached independently. The FTC says federal government sites generally use .gov and secure sites use https. A familiar name, badge, or copied page is not enough to establish that a communication came from the agency. [S1][S2]

Also verify the exact claim being made. The October announcement reports 19 removed websites and more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since 2024; it does not provide an individual eligibility test or say that a consumer must transfer money to receive redress. If a supposed FTC representative asks you to move money, withdraw cash, or buy gold for protection, do not act on that demand. Use the FTC’s official Report Fraud option instead. [S1][S2]
