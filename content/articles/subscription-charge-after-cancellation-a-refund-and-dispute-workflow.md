A subscription charge after cancellation is frustrating, but it does not by itself explain what happened or guarantee a refund. Start by checking the cancellation record and the charge, then ask the company to resolve the issue. If that does not work, use your card issuer’s process for disputing a charge. The right next step depends on what you can document—and on the issuer’s instructions. [S1][S2]

## Step-by-step: check, request, escalate

### 1. Identify the charge

Find the transaction on your card statement or account and note the amount, date, and merchant name as shown. Compare it with the subscription’s billing information and the cancellation confirmation, if you have one. Keep a copy of the relevant statement entry and messages so you can describe the same transaction consistently to the company and, if needed, your issuer. The CFPB lists guides on disputing a credit-card charge and seeking a refund for a product or service purchased with a credit card. [S2]

Don’t decide what the charge means from its label alone. Check the account or receipt associated with the subscription, and compare the amount and date with what you expected to be billed. Ask yourself: Is this a new charge after cancellation, a charge you did not recognize, or a payment you expected but want refunded? Those are different descriptions of the problem, and being precise helps you make a clear request.

### 2. Gather the cancellation details

Look for the date and time you cancelled, how you did it, and any confirmation shown on screen or sent by email. Save the confirmation and any support messages. If you do not have a confirmation, write down what you remember—including the account or email address you used and the cancellation route you tried—while the details are fresh. This is preparation for explaining the situation, not proof that the company received or processed a cancellation.

Review the terms you saw when you signed up and any subscription or cancellation information still available in your account. Pay attention to whether the offer was a trial, when it was scheduled to renew, and what the terms said about cancelling. The FTC warns that a “free trial” can lead to a charge if you do not cancel on time, and advises consumers to understand the trial terms and mark a reminder before the trial ends. [S1]

### 3. Contact the company with a specific request

Use the company’s account support or another contact method it provides. State the essential facts in a short sequence: the subscription you cancelled, when and how you cancelled it, the charge you are asking about, and what outcome you are requesting. For example: “I cancelled this subscription on [date]. I was charged [amount] on [date]. Please confirm the subscription’s status, explain this charge, and tell me whether you will refund it.”

Ask for two separate things: confirmation that the subscription is no longer active, and an explanation or refund decision for the charge already made. Cancelling addresses future subscription activity; a refund request addresses money already charged. Do not assume that one request automatically resolves the other. The FTC’s subscription advice distinguishes stopping a subscription from dealing with charges, and points consumers to steps for stopping payments when they cannot cancel. [S1]

Keep the message factual and avoid including unnecessary personal or payment information. Save what you send and the company’s reply, including any case or reference number it gives you. If you speak by phone, note the date, the contact method, and what the representative said. These records make it easier to explain the same issue if you later contact your card issuer.

### 4. Check whether the subscription is still active

After contacting the company, check the account for a cancellation status or a response confirming that the subscription ended. If you cannot find one, ask the company directly whether the subscription is cancelled and what, if anything, remains to be done. Keep a record of the answer. Do not treat a missing confirmation as proof either that cancellation succeeded or that it failed; get the status clarified.

If you are still trying to cancel, follow the company’s stated cancellation route and save evidence of each attempt. The FTC notes that some businesses make cancellation difficult and advises consumers who cannot cancel to call their credit-card company and ask it to stop the payments. [S1] That guidance does not tell you what a particular card issuer will do, so ask the issuer what options apply to your account and how to proceed.

### 5. If the company does not resolve it, contact your card issuer

If the company does not explain or resolve the charge, contact the issuer using the contact information or account tools it provides. Describe the issue accurately: you cancelled and are questioning a later subscription charge, or you tried to cancel but could not complete the process. Ask how to dispute this particular charge and what information the issuer needs. The CFPB provides a how-to guide for disputing a charge on a credit-card bill, but the evidence here does not specify the steps, deadlines, or outcome for an individual dispute. Follow your issuer’s instructions rather than relying on a general timeline. [S2]

Give the issuer the records relevant to your account: the transaction details, cancellation confirmation or notes about your attempt, and the company’s response. Explain whether you contacted the company and what happened. Keep copies of anything you submit and note when and how you contacted the issuer. If the issuer asks for more information, respond through its stated process and keep a copy of your response.

Be careful to distinguish a subscription charge you recognize but believe occurred after cancellation from a charge you do not recognize at all. Do not call a charge “unauthorized” just because you are seeking a refund; explain what you know and let the issuer tell you how it categorizes the matter. The CFPB’s overview discusses unauthorized use in the context of a card reported lost or stolen before it is used. That limited statement does not establish how a post-cancellation subscription charge will be treated. [S2]

## Checklist: make a clean record

Before you contact the company or issuer, gather what you can. You do not need to have every item to ask a question, but a concise record can help you explain the issue:

- The merchant name, charge amount, and transaction date.
- The subscription or account involved, including the email or username used to sign up.
- The cancellation date, method, and any confirmation or on-screen message.
- The subscription terms or billing information you can still access.
- The messages you sent to the company and its replies.
- The outcome you want: confirmation of cancellation, an explanation, a refund, or instructions for disputing the charge.
- Notes from calls, including when you called and any reference number provided.

Use the same dates and description when you speak with the company and the issuer. If a detail is uncertain, say it is uncertain rather than filling in a gap. For example, distinguish “I clicked cancel and saw a confirmation” from “I tried to cancel but do not know whether it went through.” A precise account is more useful than a stronger claim you cannot support.

### If you signed up through a trial

Check the offer’s terms before deciding whether the charge conflicts with what you agreed to. The FTC says trial offers may require a payment card so a business can charge the consumer if cancellation does not happen before the trial ends. It recommends understanding the terms and setting a reminder to cancel before the trial ends. [S1]

If the charge is connected to a trial and you missed the cancellation point, you can still ask the company to review the charge and explain its refund decision. Do not describe a missed deadline as a successful cancellation. If you did cancel, give the company the date and whatever confirmation you have, and ask it to explain why it charged you. A request may be denied; neither the cited material nor this workflow promises a refund. [S1]

### If you cannot get the company to stop charging

The FTC advises consumers who cannot cancel to call their credit-card company and ask it to stop the payments. [S1] When you call, explain whether you are unable to complete cancellation, have already cancelled and received another charge, or have both problems. Ask what the issuer can do to stop future payments and what separate step is needed to dispute an existing charge. Do not assume that asking to stop future payments also decides whether a past charge will be refunded.

If the company’s cancellation process is difficult, keep a dated record of the route you tried and the result. If the available process gives you no confirmation, contact the company and ask for written confirmation of the subscription’s status. The FTC describes businesses that make cancellation difficult and continue charging consumers, but that does not establish what happened in a particular account. [S1]

### If you still need help

The CFPB’s credit-card page links to guides about disputing a charge and getting a refund for a credit-card purchase. Use those resources and your issuer’s own instructions to identify the appropriate next step for your situation. [S2] The FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud, but a report is not a substitute for asking the company about a charge or contacting your card issuer about a card dispute. [S3]

Keep expectations measured. A complaint or dispute is a way to present the facts and ask for review; it is not a guarantee of a particular decision. The materials cited here do not establish a universal deadline, required evidence list, or refund entitlement for a subscription charge after cancellation. Check the current instructions that apply to your card and account before acting. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Confirm the amount, date, and merchant shown on the account.
- **The cancellation:** Check the date, method, and any confirmation; if the status is unclear, ask the company.
- **The offer:** Review the trial or renewal terms you can access before describing why the charge is wrong. [S1]
- **The remedy:** Ask separately about ending the subscription, stopping future payments, and requesting a refund for the charge already made. [S1]
- **The dispute process:** Confirm the issuer’s requirements and timing for this charge. The supplied CFPB page links to a dispute guide but does not provide those details. [S2]
- **The wording:** Describe what you know, what you attempted, and what remains uncertain; do not claim a cancellation or unauthorized transaction that you cannot substantiate.

A useful sequence is simple: document the charge, confirm what happened with cancellation, ask the company for an explanation and a specific remedy, then contact the card issuer if the issue remains unresolved. Keep the records together and follow the instructions for your own account. That approach does not guarantee a refund, but it gives each party a clear account of the problem and the action you are requesting. [S1][S2]
