## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency announced those figures on October 29, 2026, as part of a summary of its ongoing work to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The FTC described impersonation scams as a persistent problem: its Bureau of Consumer Protection director said they had been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That statement describes reports overall; it does not establish that any particular person who encountered a scam lost money, or that a specific victim is eligible for redress. [S1]

The announcement is a record of agency activity, not a notice that consumers should expect a payment. The more than $80 million figure refers to consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions since the rule took effect; the extract does not identify individual cases, recipients, payment schedules, or how consumers can check whether they qualify. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

An official-looking call, message, or website is not proof that the FTC—or another organization—is really contacting you. The FTC’s consumer advice page gives a concrete warning: the agency will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. If a caller claiming to be from the FTC makes one of those demands, treat it as a scam rather than complying. [S2]

A useful decision path is to focus first on what the person wants you to do. If they claim to be an agency or organization and pressure you to move money, hand over cash or gold, or act under threat, stop before taking that step. Do not let a caller’s claimed title or an official-looking page outweigh the warning signs in the request. The FTC’s stated warning specifically covers threats and instructions to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy and give away gold. [S2]

Next, separate the claim from the evidence. A caller saying that the FTC has found money for you, or that you must pay or transfer money to resolve a matter, is not proof of an official case or payment. The agency’s report of redress is an aggregate statement about enforcement actions, and the supplied announcement does not say that the FTC contacts consumers with those payment claims. [S1][S2]

If the approach involves a charity or a supposed sweepstakes prize, check the FTC’s consumer alert about charity impersonators before sharing information or following instructions. The alert says scammers may impersonate reputable charities and may lead people to believe they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. The available alert excerpt poses how to tell whether a prize is real as a question, but does not provide specific verification steps, so do not treat a caller’s assurance as confirmation. [S2]

If you believe someone tried to defraud you, the FTC’s consumer advice site provides a “Report Fraud” option. The supplied information does not specify what happens after a report or promise that reporting will recover money, so treat reporting as a way to notify the agency—not as a guarantee of reimbursement or a particular outcome. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message, click, call, or payment demand, use this short checklist:

- **Identify the demand.** Is the person threatening you, telling you to move money to protect it, or asking you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over? Those are actions the FTC specifically warns it will not demand. Stop rather than comply. [S2]
- **Check the source of a claimed payment.** The FTC’s reported $80 million-plus in redress is tied to enforcement actions, but the announcement excerpt does not provide a list of eligible consumers or a process for claiming money. Do not infer eligibility from the headline or from a message that uses the FTC’s name. [S1]
- **Treat prize claims cautiously.** If someone claims you won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes, consult the FTC alert on charity impersonators. The extract does not give a complete prize-verification procedure, so avoid relying on instructions or links supplied by the person making the claim. [S2]
- **Choose a clear next step.** If the contact matches the FTC’s warning signs, do not transfer or hand over money. If you suspect fraud, use the FTC consumer site’s fraud-reporting option. Do not assume a report means a refund, payment, or legal result. [S2]

The FTC’s figures show enforcement activity, while the consumer guidance offers a practical rule for an immediate decision: scrutinize demands to move money, give away cash or gold, or act under threat. Keep those two points distinct—a public enforcement total is not an individual eligibility notice, and a scam report is not a promise of recovery. [S1][S2]
