A payment processor is rarely the name a consumer remembers after a purchase. But the company handling a transaction can matter when a merchant is accused of fraud. The Federal Trade Commission says global processor Nuvei and its subsidiaries opened and maintained accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle the FTC’s charges and must implement robust merchant-screening practices. [S1]

For consumers, the key distinction is between an enforcement settlement and an individual reimbursement. The announcement describes a settlement between the FTC and Nuvei; the supplied information does not say that consumers will receive money from the $4.85 million or explain a process for claiming it. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s case targets the payment processor’s role in serving merchants—not just the conduct of the merchants themselves. The agency alleges Nuvei opened and maintained processing accounts for businesses engaged in deception, including tech support scams. Its concern, as described in the announcement, is that Nuvei knew or should have known about that activity. [S1]

The settlement requires Nuvei to use robust merchant-screening practices and pay $4.85 million. The FTC’s announcement says the company and its subsidiaries were alleged to have knowingly processed payments for merchants engaged in fraudulent activity; the headline and summary describe the resolution as settling FTC charges. [S1]

That matters because a payment can involve several businesses: the seller a person interacts with and the processor that helps the seller accept payments. The FTC’s action focuses on the processor’s alleged relationship with merchants, including whether it should have recognized deceptive activity. It does not, in the supplied details, identify a particular consumer, merchant, transaction date, or payment method. [S1]

The allegation is not the same as a statement that every Nuvei-processed transaction was fraudulent. The FTC describes accounts tied to merchants it alleges were deceptive, including tech support scams; the supplied announcement does not list all merchants or say that every customer who paid one of them is entitled to a refund. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a charge looks connected to a tech support offer or another unfamiliar seller, start with the transaction itself rather than assuming the processor’s name identifies the seller. Compare the amount and date with your receipts, emails, or order records, and note the business name shown on the charge. This is a practical way to organize what you know; the FTC announcement does not provide transaction-level records or a lookup tool for identifying Nuvei-related payments. [S1]

If you believe you paid a scammer, the FTC’s consumer site offers scam advice and a way to report fraud. A report can give the agency information about a suspected scam, but the supplied materials do not say that filing a report will recover money or resolve a particular payment dispute. [S2]

Keep any records that help explain what happened: the charge, the seller’s name, messages, receipts, and a short account of what the seller promised. If you contact the seller or another company about the payment, stick to the details you can document. The sources provided do not specify payment-dispute procedures, deadlines, or legal rights, so do not treat this settlement announcement as instructions about how to pursue a claim. [S1][S2]

The settlement also does not establish that consumers should stop using a particular payment method or treat every tech support service as fraudulent. The FTC’s announcement concerns allegations about Nuvei’s processing accounts for merchants engaged in deception; it does not provide a list of legitimate or illegitimate sellers. [S1]

A useful decision path is: identify the charge; check whether you recognize the seller and the purchase; preserve the records if you do not; then consult the FTC’s consumer resources if you suspect fraud. If the charge is not connected to the merchants described in the FTC’s case, the announcement alone cannot confirm whether it is covered or what remedy may apply. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The seller:** Does the transaction record name the business you dealt with, or only a company involved in processing the payment? The FTC announcement does not name every merchant involved. [S1]
- **The connection to the case:** Does your evidence link the charge to a merchant or conduct described in the announcement? The supplied details identify tech support scams as an example but do not provide a complete merchant list or transaction search. [S1]
- **The money:** Is there an official notice saying you qualify for a payment and explaining how to claim it? The supplied announcement says Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle FTC charges; it does not say that consumers will receive checks or describe a refund program. [S1]
- **The next step:** If you suspect a scam, consult the FTC’s consumer advice and fraud-reporting resources. Verify any instructions through an official FTC site rather than relying on an unsolicited message claiming to arrange a refund. [S2]

Until you can confirm those details, treat the settlement as information about the FTC’s action—not proof that a specific charge qualifies for reimbursement. The practical priority is to document what happened, check the seller and transaction, and use official consumer resources to decide what to do next. [S1][S2]
