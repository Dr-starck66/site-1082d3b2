The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei and related companies will pay $4.85 million to settle charges that they processed payments for merchants involved in deception, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. The settlement also requires stronger merchant screening practices. For consumers, the announcement is a reason to check a suspicious transaction carefully—not proof that a particular charge is connected to the case or that a refund is coming. [S1]

## What changed

In a September 4, 2026 announcement, the FTC said Nuvei opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deceptive activity. The FTC’s complaint alleges that the activity included tech support scams. These are allegations described by the agency, not a finding in the supplied announcement about any individual consumer’s payment. [S1]

Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle the FTC’s charges and must implement robust merchant screening practices, according to the agency. The supplied announcement does not specify how the settlement money will be distributed, identify individual consumers who may be affected, or provide a process or deadline for consumers to claim money. Don’t treat the settlement amount as a promised payment to you. [S1]

That distinction matters if you recognize a business name from the news but cannot match it to a specific transaction. A processor can handle payments for merchants; the FTC’s announcement concerns Nuvei’s alleged role in processing payments for merchants accused of deception. It does not, in the material provided, identify consumers’ account statements, list merchant names, or say that every Nuvei-processed payment involved wrongdoing. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Start with the transaction, not the headline. If you see a charge you do not recognize, check the date and amount against receipts, order confirmations, subscription notices, and messages. Write down the exact description shown on your statement. Then contact your bank, card issuer, or payment service through its official app, website, or the number on your card to ask how to report or question the transaction. This is a practical next step; the FTC announcement does not set out a consumer claim process for this settlement. [S1]

If you do recognize the merchant but believe you were misled, keep copies of what was promised and what you received, along with payment records and any attempts to resolve the matter. Explain the specific problem when you contact the payment provider or merchant. The FTC announcement describes alleged deceptive merchant activity but does not establish that any particular purchase was fraudulent, or say what remedy an individual consumer will receive. [S1]

If a charge later becomes the subject of a debt-collection contact, treat that as a separate issue. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information on how to dispute the debt, and more; if the collector will not or cannot provide this information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. Ask for the details and check whether the debt is yours rather than assuming that the Nuvei news proves or disproves it. [S3]

If you suspect the problem has affected your credit file, review your credit reports for unfamiliar or incorrect entries. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year and says requesting them will not hurt your credit score. Its resources also explain how to dispute a credit-report error. A payment-processing dispute by itself is not evidence that your credit report contains an error, so focus on entries you can identify and verify. [S2]

A useful decision path is: **unrecognized transaction → ask your payment provider how to report it; recognized purchase but suspected deception → gather the record and describe what was misleading; collection contact → request and verify the debt details; credit-report entry you believe is wrong → review the report and use the dispute process.** The right branch depends on what actually happened to your account; the settlement announcement does not answer that for you. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Before sharing account details or responding to a message about a refund or debt, verify who contacted you using a phone number or website you locate independently. Compare the transaction’s date, amount, and statement description with your own records. If contacted about a debt, check the creditor, amount, and dispute information the CFPB says a collector must provide. If you are considering disputing a credit-report entry, first locate the entry and identify what you believe is inaccurate. [S2][S3]

Keep the case’s limits in view: the announcement reports a settlement of FTC charges, names no individual consumer payment, and—based on the supplied details—does not give consumers a claim deadline or promise a refund. The CFPB’s credit-report and debt-collection resources offer next steps for those specific problems, including a way to submit a complaint and a consumer helpline at 855-411-2372. Use those resources for the issue they cover; they are not described as a Nuvei settlement payout channel. [S1][S2][S3]
