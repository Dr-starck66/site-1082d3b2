A payment processor can become part of a consumer-fraud story even when the consumer’s complaint is about a merchant. The Federal Trade Commission says global processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The FTC’s announcement describes allegations about the company’s business practices; it does not announce payments to affected consumers. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC alleged that Nuvei Corporation and subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception. The agency specifically cited tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. Those are allegations in the FTC’s case, not details that establish what happened in any particular consumer’s transaction. [S1]

Under the settlement described by the FTC, Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and must implement robust merchant-screening practices. The supplied announcement does not give the full screening requirements, say how the payment will be used, or provide a schedule for any consumer payment. [S1]

That distinction matters: a settlement about a processor’s merchant controls is not, by itself, a notice that a particular merchant was involved or that a particular charge qualifies for reimbursement. The FTC’s announcement identifies alleged processing for deceptive merchants, including tech-support scams, but the supplied text does not name individual merchants or affected consumers. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are trying to make sense of a suspicious payment, start with the transaction itself rather than assuming the settlement applies to it. Compare the charge with the merchant name, amount, and date shown in your account records, and keep the receipt or other records you already have. The FTC’s announcement concerns Nuvei’s alleged processing relationships; it does not say that every charge handled by the company was fraudulent. [S1]

If the issue is a tech-support offer or charge, the FTC’s case is relevant context: the agency says the alleged merchant fraud included tech-support scams that took millions from consumers. But the announcement does not identify a specific scam, describe the steps a consumer should take to dispute a charge, or guarantee a refund. Avoid treating the settlement amount as a fund available to claim unless an official notice says so. [S1]

A charge you do not recognize and a debt-collection contact are different problems. If a debt collector contacts you, the CFPB says you can use the contact to find out about the debt and determine whether you owe it. A collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt, among other information; if the collector will not or cannot provide it, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

If you are concerned that a financial problem or fraud has affected your credit record, review your credit reports for errors. The CFPB recommends checking them at least once a year and says requesting your reports will not hurt your credit score. It also provides a list of credit reporting companies so you can request and review consumer reports. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Use this decision path before relying on a claim about the settlement or responding to a related contact:

- **You suspect a charge is fraudulent:** Check the transaction details against your own records. The FTC announcement describes allegations involving Nuvei’s processing for deceptive merchants, but does not identify individual charges or set out consumer refund procedures. [S1]
- **Someone says you are owed money from the case:** Look for an official notice that identifies the payment program, who may qualify, and how to verify it. The supplied FTC announcement states the settlement amount but does not say that consumers will receive money or describe a claims process. [S1]
- **A debt collector contacts you:** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount owed, and information on how to dispute the debt. The CFPB says a collector is required to provide this information, among other details. [S3]
- **You see an unfamiliar item on a credit report:** Review the report and use the CFPB’s guidance on disputing an error. The CFPB says consumers have the right to see and act on the data in their reports, and provides a guide to disputing errors. [S2]

Before sharing personal or payment information, verify who is contacting you and what they are asking you to do. The FTC source supplied here is a press release about the Nuvei settlement; it does not establish that an unsolicited message, caller, or website claiming an affiliation with the case is genuine. For a credit-report concern, go directly to the CFPB’s credit-report resources; for a debt-collection concern, consult its guidance on communicating with collectors. [S1][S2][S3]
