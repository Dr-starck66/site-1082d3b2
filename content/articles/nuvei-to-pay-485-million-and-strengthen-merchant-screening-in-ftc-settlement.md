The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and adopt stronger merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The agency alleged Nuvei opened and maintained accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaging in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

The announcement is a reminder that the company processing a payment may not be the same business that sold a product or service. But the FTC’s announcement does not say that consumers will receive money from this settlement, or describe a process for individual customers to seek compensation. [S1]

## What changed

In a September 4, 2026, announcement, the FTC said Nuvei and its subsidiaries would pay $4.85 million to settle the agency’s charges and would have to implement robust merchant-screening practices. The complaint alleged that the processor opened and maintained accounts for merchants involved in deception, including tech-support scams. These are allegations described by the FTC; the supplied announcement does not provide further settlement terms or say that consumers will receive a share of the payment. [S1]

For consumers, the key distinction is between the payment processor and the merchant. Nuvei’s role in processing payments is the focus of the FTC action, while the alleged deception was attributed to merchants using its services. The announcement therefore should not be read as a finding that every transaction handled by a processor is fraudulent, or as a guarantee that a particular customer will get money back. [S1]

The FTC’s stated remedy concerns screening merchants. The available announcement does not explain how the new practices will work, name every affected merchant, identify individual transactions, or set out a consumer claim deadline. Consumers should avoid assuming that the settlement automatically changes a disputed charge, debt, or credit-file entry. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a charge or service seems connected to a suspected scam, start by sorting out what kind of problem you have. Write down the merchant name shown on the statement, the transaction date and amount, and what was promised or delivered. Keep receipts, emails, and other records together. This gives you a clearer basis to check the transaction and explain the issue when contacting the relevant company. The FTC announcement itself does not provide an individual refund procedure. [S1]

If the issue involves a debt collector rather than a payment or merchant dispute, the CFPB says a collector is required to provide information including the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt. If you believe you do not owe it, the CFPB advises telling the collector; avoiding contact is unlikely to make the collector stop. Do not treat a merchant dispute as proof that a separate debt is invalid—first identify who is contacting you and what the debt is said to be for. [S3]

If you are worried that fraud has affected your credit record, check your credit reports for unfamiliar or incorrect information. The CFPB recommends reviewing reports at least once a year, says requesting them will not hurt your credit score, and provides guidance on disputing errors. A payment-processor settlement alone does not establish that a specific entry on your report is wrong; compare any entry with your own account records. [S2]

For an escalation route, the FTC site in the announcement includes a fraud-reporting option. The CFPB pages also provide a complaint option and consumer guidance, including material on debt collection and credit-report errors. Choose the channel that matches the issue: suspected fraud, a collector’s demand, and a credit-report error are different problems. A complaint is a way to raise an issue, not a promise of reimbursement or a particular outcome. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Confirm the date, amount, and merchant name on your account statement. Check those details against receipts and any messages about the purchase. [S1]
- **The responsible business:** Determine whether your concern is with the merchant, a payment shown on your account, a debt collector, or information on a credit report. The Nuvei announcement concerns merchant screening and FTC charges; it does not identify every merchant or customer involved. [S1]
- **The kind of claim:** If someone says you owe a debt, ask for the creditor, amount, and dispute information described by the CFPB. If your concern is a credit-report entry, review the report and use the CFPB’s error-dispute guidance. [S2][S3]
- **What the settlement actually offers:** The FTC announcement states a $4.85 million payment and stronger screening practices, but the supplied text does not describe consumer payouts, an application process, or a deadline to file a claim. Verify any claim about eligibility or payment with an official source before sharing personal or financial information. [S1]

The practical takeaway is to use the Nuvei case as context, not as proof that a particular charge qualifies for relief. Identify the transaction or contact at issue, preserve the records, and follow the official guidance for that specific problem. [S1][S2][S3]
