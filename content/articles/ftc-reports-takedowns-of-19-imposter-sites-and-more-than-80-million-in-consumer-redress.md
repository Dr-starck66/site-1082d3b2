The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement comes as imposter scams remain a major source of reported fraud: the FTC says consumers have reported billions of dollars in losses, and its consumer advice site reports more than 1 million imposter-scam reports and $3.5 billion in reported losses last year. [S1][S2]

Those figures describe reports and enforcement outcomes; they do not mean every person who encounters an impersonator will get money back. For consumers, the most immediate use of the announcement is as a warning sign: a message claiming to be from the FTC is not trustworthy just because it uses the agency’s name, logo, or a convincing-looking website. [S1][S2]

## What changed

The FTC highlighted its efforts to combat impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 websites posing as the agency and enforcement actions that resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The agency also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions in losses. [S1]

Separately, FTC consumer advice says that last year people made more than 1 million reports about imposter scams and reported losing $3.5 billion. The figures are a snapshot of reports and reported losses—not a count of every scam or a guarantee that a person who reports a loss will recover it. [S2]

The agency’s practical warning is specific: the FTC will not threaten you, tell you to move money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. A demand like that is a scam, even if the caller or website claims to represent the government. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

If a caller, text, email, or website says it is the FTC, focus on what it is asking you to do—not on how official it looks. A threat, an urgent demand to transfer money, or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone are clear reasons to stop. Do not follow those instructions. [S2]

If the contact does not include one of those demands, that alone does not establish that it is genuine. The FTC says scammers use fake FTC websites, and it has reported taking down 19 such sites. Treat the agency’s name or appearance as something a scammer can imitate, not proof of identity. [S1][S2]

A useful decision path is:

- **Asked to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold for someone?** Stop. The FTC says it will not make those demands. [S2]
- **Threatened or pressured while someone claims to be the FTC?** Treat the contact as a scam warning, not an instruction to comply. [S2]
- **Looking at a website that claims to be the FTC?** Don’t rely on its branding alone. Use the official FTC site or its consumer advice page as your starting point instead of following links in the suspicious message. [S1][S2]
- **Already paid or shared information?** The FTC’s consumer site directs people to report fraud and offers advice on what to do after a scam. Use those official resources to identify next steps; the agency’s enforcement figures do not promise a refund or other outcome in an individual case. [S1][S2]

The takedowns and redress show that the FTC is pursuing impersonation scams, but they are not a real-time verification service for every call or website. The safest response to a suspicious demand is to pause and consult the agency’s own consumer information, rather than letting an unexpected contact set the terms or deadline. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding, check the request itself: Is someone threatening you, telling you to move money for protection, or asking for cash or gold? The FTC says it will not do these things. If so, do not comply. [S2]

If you are unsure whether a site or message is genuine, independently open the FTC’s official website or consumer advice page rather than using a link supplied by the contact. The agency’s own materials warn about fake FTC websites and provide a Report Fraud option. [S1][S2]

If you have already acted, use the FTC’s official fraud-reporting and consumer-advice resources to consider what to do next. Keep expectations grounded: the agency’s announcement reports more than $80 million in redress from enforcement actions, but it does not say that every reported loss is eligible for payment or that reporting guarantees recovery. [S1][S2]
