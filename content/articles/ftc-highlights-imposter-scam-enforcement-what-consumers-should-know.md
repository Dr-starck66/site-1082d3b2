## What changed

On October 29, the Federal Trade Commission highlighted its efforts to fight impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 websites posing as the FTC and enforcement actions resulting in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the agency’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. Those are agency-wide enforcement and reporting figures—not a promise that any individual scam victim will receive money back. [S1]

The announcement arrives as the FTC’s consumer advice pages warn about impersonators using different stories and channels. Recent alerts describe scammers posing as charities and claiming someone has won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes, and impersonators placing ads that appear to come from familiar businesses or government agencies. The details change, but the pressure to trust a message, click, or pay can be the common thread. [S2]

The FTC’s basic warning is especially important when someone claims to be from the agency: it will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to another person. A caller, text, email, or ad claiming otherwise is not made legitimate by an official-sounding name, logo, or urgent explanation. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

The enforcement figures show that the FTC is taking action against impersonation, but they do not let consumers identify a message as real or recover a particular loss. Treat the announcement as context, not a verification tool: the key decision is whether the person contacting you is asking you to do something risky before you independently confirm who they are. [S1][S2]

Use a simple decision path. First, pause if a message demands immediate action or asks you to move money, hand over cash or gold, or share sensitive information. The FTC specifically identifies threats and instructions to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy and deliver gold as signs of an FTC impersonation scam. Don’t use contact details or links supplied by the person making the demand to verify the story. [S2]

Second, check the claim through a route you choose yourself. If someone says a charity has selected you for a prize, contact the charity using contact information you already trust and ask whether the sweepstakes and the caller are genuine. The FTC warns that scammers may impersonate reputable charities and make donors believe they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. [S2]

Third, distinguish a suspicious contact from a situation where you have already acted. If you have paid someone you suspect is a scammer, or shared personal information or access to a device, the FTC’s consumer advice site has a “What To Do if You Were Scammed” article addressing those situations. The site also offers ways to report fraud and identity theft. [S2]

Keep the original message and note how the person contacted you, what they claimed, and what they asked you to do. That record can help you explain the situation when seeking help or making a report. This is a practical precaution, not a guarantee of recovery or a substitute for advice tailored to your circumstances.

The FTC’s $80 million figure should not be read as a fund that every victim can claim from. The announcement describes consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions since the rule took effect; it does not say that all victims qualify, give a universal application process, or promise a payout to people who report a scam. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message, run through these checks:

- **What are they asking for?** Stop if the contact claims to be the FTC and threatens you or directs you to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold and give it to someone. [S2]
- **Can you confirm it independently?** For a supposed charity prize, reach the charity using contact information you find independently, not details supplied in the message. The FTC says scammers use charity identities and sweepstakes claims to deceive people. [S2]
- **Have you already shared money, information, or device access?** Use the FTC’s consumer advice on what to do after a scam and consider its fraud or identity-theft reporting options, as relevant to what happened. [S2]
- **Is someone promising a refund because of the FTC announcement?** Verify the claim separately. The announcement reports enforcement outcomes; it does not establish that a particular person is eligible for redress. [S1]

If you’re unsure, don’t let urgency make the decision for you. Stop the exchange, verify the claim through an independent channel, and use the FTC’s official consumer advice and reporting resources if you need to take the next step. [S2]
