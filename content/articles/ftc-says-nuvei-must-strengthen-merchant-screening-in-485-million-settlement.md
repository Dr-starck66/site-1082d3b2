A payment processor can be part of a transaction without being the business a consumer intended to pay. That distinction is at the center of an FTC settlement with global payment processor Nuvei: the agency alleged that the company and its subsidiaries opened and maintained accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaging in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

The FTC announced the settlement on September 4, 2026. Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and must implement robust merchant-screening practices, according to the agency’s announcement. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement is a reason to scrutinize a suspicious payment—not a notice that every transaction involving Nuvei was fraudulent, or that customers will automatically receive money back. Here’s how to use the news without assuming more than it establishes. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s case concerns Nuvei’s role as a payment processor. The agency alleged that Nuvei Corporation and subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants engaged in deception, including tech-support scams, and that the firms knew or should have known about the activity. These are allegations described in the FTC announcement; the settlement requires new merchant-screening practices and a $4.85 million payment. [S1]

The distinction matters because a processor handles payments for merchants, while the alleged deception was attributed to merchants using those processing accounts. The announcement does not say that Nuvei itself contacted every affected consumer, sold the services at issue, or made every charge described in the case. [S1]

The $4.85 million figure is the payment Nuvei agreed to make to settle the FTC’s charges. The announcement does not describe it as a fund for individual claims or say that every consumer is entitled to a share. It also does not provide, in the available details, instructions for filing a claim or a list of affected customers. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are looking into a charge, start with the transaction itself rather than the company name in this headline. Check the date and amount against your receipts, subscriptions, online orders, and messages. Ask whether you recognize the product or service and the business that sold it. A payment processor’s involvement alone does not establish that a transaction was deceptive. [S1]

If you do not recognize the purchase, or the seller’s explanation does not match what you paid for, keep the records together: the charge details, receipt or order confirmation, relevant messages, and any response from the seller. Write down what you expected to buy, what you received, and when you tried to resolve the issue. This gives you a clearer account if you choose to report the matter.

Then choose the next step based on what you find. If the charge matches a purchase and the seller resolves your concern, keep the records. If you believe you paid a scammer or were misled, the FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud; its advice section also includes a resource on what to do if you were scammed. Reporting is an option, not a guarantee of reimbursement or a particular outcome. [S2]

If you are trying to determine whether your experience relates to the FTC’s case, avoid treating the settlement announcement as confirmation. The FTC’s description refers to merchants and alleged tech-support scams, but the available announcement details do not identify individual consumers or specific transactions. Compare your own records with the description and report suspected fraud through the FTC’s consumer site if appropriate. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Confirm the exact date, amount, and seller using your own account records and purchase documents. Do not assume a payment is connected to this case just because Nuvei’s name appears in material you find about it.
- **The seller’s explanation:** Ask what the payment was for and compare the answer with what you ordered or agreed to buy. Keep the reply and any supporting records.
- **The settlement’s limits:** The announced $4.85 million is a company payment to settle FTC charges. The announcement does not promise a consumer payout or set out an individual claim process in the details available here. [S1]
- **Where to report:** If you believe you paid a scammer, use the FTC consumer site’s fraud-reporting option and review its guidance for people who were scammed. Do not interpret a report as a promise of a refund. [S2]

The practical takeaway is to separate three questions: Do you recognize the transaction? Does the seller’s account of it match your records? Do you have a concrete reason to suspect deception? Answering those questions can help you decide whether to seek clarification, preserve evidence, or report suspected fraud—without treating a processor’s name or a company settlement as proof about your own payment.
