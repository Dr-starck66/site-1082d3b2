## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites that impersonated the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency described those figures as highlights of its ongoing work to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The announcement is a reminder that a convincing government name or official-looking website is not proof that a message is genuine. The FTC says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That describes reports and reported losses—not a guarantee that every report is verified or that a particular consumer can recover money. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer advice draws a clear line around a purported contact from the agency: it will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. A demand like that is a scam warning, not a reason to comply quickly. [S2]

Impersonators may also claim to represent organizations other than the government. The FTC recently warned about scammers pretending to be charities, including callers who tell donors they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. The warning is a useful reminder to assess the request itself, not just whether you recognize the organization’s name. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

The FTC’s enforcement figures show agency action, but they do not establish that a specific website, caller, or message has been investigated—or that an individual will receive money. Treat the announcement as context, not as confirmation that a person contacting you is legitimate or that you qualify for redress. [S1]

If someone says they are from the FTC and pressures you to move money, withdraw cash, or buy gold for safekeeping, stop before taking those steps. Do not let a threat or a claim of urgency replace your own verification. The FTC specifically says it does not make those demands. [S2]

For a charity or sweepstakes call, separate two questions: Did you actually enter or donate in a way that could explain the contact, and is the caller asking you to do something before you can confirm the prize independently? The FTC’s alert identifies these calls as a way scammers can make a supposed prize seem connected to a charity you support. If the story does not check out, do not proceed on the caller’s instructions. [S2]

A practical decision path is:

- **Is the contact claiming to be the FTC?** If it threatens you, tells you to transfer funds to protect them, or asks you to hand over cash or gold, do not follow that instruction. [S2]
- **Is it a sweepstakes or charity claim?** Pause and assess whether the claim matches something you actually did; a familiar charity name alone is not confirmation. [S2]
- **Are you still unsure?** Do not decide based on the incoming call or message alone. Look for the FTC’s consumer advice through its official consumer site, and use its fraud-reporting option if you believe you encountered a scam. [S2]

Reporting is an escalation option, not a promise of an investigation or repayment. The FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud and links to advice about what to do if you paid someone you suspect is a scammer or shared personal information. If money or information has already changed hands, consult that advice promptly and describe what happened accurately. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message, call, or website, check the exact claim and the requested action—not just the logo, agency name, or caller’s confidence. For an alleged FTC contact, compare the demand with the agency’s stated warning signs: threats, instructions to transfer money for protection, or requests to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone are not legitimate FTC instructions. [S2]

For an alleged charity prize, verify whether you entered the sweepstakes and whether the caller’s explanation makes sense before sharing information or following directions. The FTC’s alert specifically warns that scammers may connect a supposed prize to a charity a person supports; the alert does not say that every charity-related call is fraudulent. [S2]

Finally, keep the announcement’s numbers in perspective. The FTC says 19 impersonation websites were removed and enforcement actions produced more than $80 million in consumer redress since the rule took effect in 2024. Those aggregate figures do not say whether a particular person is eligible for money, how to apply, or whether any new payment is available. Verify any such claim using official FTC consumer information rather than a message that asks you to act under pressure. [S1][S2]
