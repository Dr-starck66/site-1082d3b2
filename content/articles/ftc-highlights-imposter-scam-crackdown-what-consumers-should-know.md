The Federal Trade Commission says it stopped 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The figures are part of an October 29 update on efforts to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The announcement is a reminder that a convincing government name or website is not proof that a message is genuine. The FTC says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC highlighted two kinds of action: taking down 19 sites posing as the FTC, and enforcement actions that have led to more than $80 million in consumer redress since the rule went into effect in 2024. Those are agency-wide results described in the announcement; they do not establish that any particular consumer will receive money or that a specific suspicious site has been removed. [S1]

The announcement focuses on FTC impersonation, not every kind of scam. The FTC’s consumer site also groups advice on government and business impersonators, phone scams, gift card scams, wire transfer scams, and other topics, but the supplied update does not give results for those categories. [S1][S2]

For consumers, a key point is that an impostor may use the FTC’s name to make a demand sound official. The FTC’s warning is direct: it will not threaten you, say you must transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

If a caller, text, email, or website claims to be the FTC and pressures you to move money, stop before taking action. Don’t transfer funds, withdraw cash, or buy gold because the person says it is necessary to protect your money; those are precisely the demands the FTC identifies as a scam warning sign. [S2]

Use a simple decision path: first, look at what the message is asking you to do. A threat or demand to move money is a reason to disengage rather than comply. Next, check the agency’s information by going to the FTC’s official site yourself instead of relying on a link, number, or instructions supplied in the message. The FTC site identifies itself as a .gov website and provides consumer advice and reporting links. [S2]

If the contact does not ask you to move money, that alone does not prove it is legitimate. Avoid treating a familiar logo, agency name, or professional tone as verification. The FTC’s update documents fake websites using its identity, while the consumer site’s warning describes specific payment demands the agency says it will not make. [S1][S2]

If you already paid someone you suspect is a scammer, or shared personal information or access to your computer or phone, the FTC’s consumer site lists a “What To Do if You Were Scammed” resource covering those situations. It also offers links to report fraud and identity theft. Use the relevant FTC guidance to identify next steps for your situation; the materials supplied here do not promise a refund or specify an outcome for an individual report. [S2]

The reported redress figure should also be read narrowly. It describes results from enforcement actions, not a new general payment program or automatic reimbursement for anyone contacted by an impostor. The FTC announcement does not provide an application process or say that consumers should pay a fee to claim redress. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message that claims to be from the FTC:

- **Check the request, not just the branding.** A threat or instruction to transfer money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and give it to someone conflicts with the FTC’s stated warning. Don’t follow those instructions. [S2]
- **Go to the agency’s site independently.** Use the FTC’s official consumer site rather than trusting a link or contact details in an unexpected message. The FTC identifies its website as a government .gov site. [S2]
- **Separate enforcement news from personal eligibility.** The $80 million-plus figure concerns consumer redress resulting from enforcement actions since 2024; the announcement does not say that everyone who reports a scam qualifies for payment. [S1]
- **If you shared information or paid, find the relevant FTC guidance.** The FTC site lists advice for people who paid a suspected scammer or gave one personal information or device access, as well as options to report fraud or identity theft. [S2]

The practical takeaway is to treat pressure to move money as a stop signal, verify through the FTC’s own site, and use its reporting and recovery guidance if you have already acted. The agency’s enforcement figures show actions it has taken; they are not a guarantee of protection, recovery, or a particular result in an individual case. [S1][S2]
