## A warranty claim starts with a paper trail

A product warranty is a promise to stand behind a product. The coverage can be broad or narrow, so the details matter: what is covered, for how long, and under what conditions. The Federal Trade Commission says warranty terms should be in writing and available to read before purchase, whether you shop in person or online. Keeping the terms alongside proof of purchase gives you a clearer starting point if the product develops a problem. [S1]

Think of a claim as a short chain of evidence. You want to be able to show what you bought, when and from whom; identify the warranty or other written promise you relied on; describe the problem; and keep a record of what you asked the seller or manufacturer to do. That is a practical record-keeping approach, not a guarantee that a claim will be approved. A warranty may exclude particular parts, repairs or circumstances, and the written terms determine what the company says it covers. [S1]

The most common mistake is treating “I have a warranty” as the whole file. The receipt may identify the seller and purchase date, while the warranty document explains coverage and limits. A product registration confirmation, order email or written exchange with the seller may answer other questions. Put them together rather than relying on memory or searching for records only after a product fails. The FTC notes that some warranties may require a returned registration card before warranty service qualifies, so check the specific terms instead of assuming registration is optional or automatic. [S1]

## First, identify what kind of promise you have

Before assembling a claim, distinguish a written product warranty from other promises or contracts. A written warranty is not necessarily a promise to repair every problem. The FTC advises buyers to examine the length of coverage and its limitations; a warranty can cover some situations but not others. It also says that if a specific part or repair is not listed as covered, assume it is not covered. Read the actual terms rather than relying on a general label such as “warranty included.” [S1]

A seller or manufacturer may also make a promise aloud. The FTC’s consumer warranty resource treats spoken warranties as a separate topic, but the available information here does not spell out when a spoken statement is enforceable or how it changes written terms. For a practical claim file, ask the person who made the promise to put it in writing, and preserve any email, message or other written response. Record who said what and when while the conversation is fresh, but do not treat your notes as a substitute for the written warranty. [S1]

Some promises relate to a service contract rather than the product warranty. For vehicles, the FTC distinguishes a manufacturer’s warranty from an optional auto service contract, sometimes marketed as an “extended warranty.” The manufacturer’s warranty is included in the price of a new vehicle and may cover a stated number of months or miles, whichever comes first; a service contract is optional and covers repairs or services described in that contract. The terms and prices of service contracts vary. If you are making a vehicle claim, check which document applies before asking a company to honor it. [S3]

A useful first decision is therefore: do you have a written product warranty, a separate service contract, a spoken assurance that needs confirmation, or more than one of these? Keep each document distinct. If the product is a vehicle, do not assume that an “extended warranty” advertisement refers to the same coverage as the manufacturer’s warranty. [S1][S3]

## Step-by-step checklist: build a claim file

**1. Find proof of purchase.** Start with a store receipt, online order confirmation or invoice. Save a copy somewhere you can retrieve it, and note the product name or model as shown on the record. If you cannot immediately find a receipt, search your email or shopping account and ask the seller whether it can provide a duplicate. These are record-finding steps, not a promise that a substitute document will satisfy a particular warranty’s requirements.

**2. Save the terms that applied to the purchase.** Keep the warranty as it appeared when you bought the product, including pages that describe duration, covered components, exclusions, required steps and how to request service. If you shop online, save or print the terms before they disappear from view; if you buy in a store, keep the printed warranty or ask how to access it. The FTC says warranties must be available to read before purchase online and in person, and that their details should be in writing. [S1]

**3. Check for registration requirements.** Look for any requirement to return a registration card or complete another stated step. Save the card or online confirmation and the date you submitted it. Do not infer that registration has been completed just because you received the product, or that registration is required for every warranty: the FTC says some warranties require a registration card before warranty service qualifies. [S1]

**4. Preserve written promises separately.** Save messages from the seller, manufacturer or service representative that describe coverage, a repair, replacement part or other commitment. Keep the full exchange where possible, with dates and the sender visible. If a promise was made on a call or in person, follow up with a neutral written message summarizing your understanding and ask the company to confirm or correct it. This creates a clearer record of what was discussed; it does not establish that the promise overrides the warranty.

**5. Document the problem, not just the conclusion.** Write down when you first noticed the issue, what the product did, and what happened immediately before it began. Take dated photographs or short recordings if they accurately show the defect. Keep repair estimates, service notes and messages about inspections together. Be precise: “the display goes dark after ten minutes” is more useful than “it is defective.” Avoid presenting a guess about the cause as a fact.

**6. Compare the problem with the coverage language.** Find the section that addresses the product part or repair at issue, the warranty period, and any exclusions or conditions. If the terms say the warranty covers only specified items, do not assume an unlisted repair is included. The FTC specifically advises that if a part or repair is not listed as covered, assume it is not covered. [S1]

**7. Follow the stated claim process and keep a copy.** Use the contact method and steps given in the warranty, and keep your submission and any response. If the document asks for information or a particular action, make a note of what you supplied and when. Do not send your only copy of a receipt or other important record; retain a duplicate where possible. The coverage and any required steps depend on the terms of the particular warranty. [S1]

**8. Ask for the decision in writing.** If a representative says the problem is not covered, ask which provision they relied on and request the explanation by email or letter. Keep the response with the warranty and your description of the issue. Then compare the stated reason with the terms you saved. A written explanation does not guarantee that the company will change its decision, but it helps you understand the dispute and decide what to do next.

## When the records do not line up

A missing receipt, an incomplete warranty copy or a verbal promise can make a claim harder to explain, but it does not tell you by itself whether the product is covered. First rebuild the record: search order history and email, ask the seller for a duplicate receipt, and request the warranty terms from the manufacturer or seller. For an oral assurance, identify the person, approximate date and exact wording as best you can, then ask the company to confirm its position in writing. Do not fill gaps with certainty you cannot support.

If the receipt shows one date but the warranty record or seller’s message refers to another, keep both records and explain the difference instead of altering either one. If a company says registration was required, locate any registration confirmation and the relevant warranty wording. If it says a repair is excluded, ask it to identify the provision. These questions focus the discussion on the written record without assuming that a discrepancy automatically decides the claim. [S1]

Also check whether the document you are relying on is actually a warranty. A service contract can have different terms and may cover only the repairs or services described in that contract. That distinction is especially relevant when a vehicle dealer or another seller uses “extended warranty” as a sales phrase. Keep the contract, warranty and purchase records together, but do not merge their coverage in your notes. [S3]

## If the first answer is no

A denial is a reason to organize and clarify the file, not to assume either that the company is right or that it must reverse course. Send a concise follow-up listing the purchase, the issue, the warranty language you believe applies, and the records attached. Ask the company to explain any remaining disagreement in writing. Keep the tone factual and save the response with the rest of the claim materials.

If direct contact does not resolve the question, the FTC consumer warranty page includes a section on resolving product problems and reporting problems. The FTC also advises consumers evaluating a company to search its name with terms such as “complaint” or “review,” or check with a local or state consumer protection office for information about the company. Those resources can help you decide what to investigate next; they do not promise an individual claim outcome. [S1]

For a question about legal rights or a possible lawsuit, do not infer a rule from a warranty summary alone. The FTC’s business guidance lists federal warranty-law topics, state law and alternatives to consumer lawsuits, but the available source extract does not provide enough detail to determine how those rules apply to a particular product or dispute. Consider getting advice from an appropriate consumer-protection office or qualified legal professional if the stakes warrant it. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

- Is the document a product warranty, a service contract, or both? For a vehicle, distinguish the manufacturer’s warranty from an optional auto service contract. [S3]
- Do you have proof showing what you bought, where you bought it and the purchase date?
- Have you saved the warranty terms and checked the coverage period, parts, repairs, exclusions and any registration condition? [S1]
- Is the promise you are relying on written down? If not, have you asked the seller or manufacturer to confirm it in writing?
- Does your description explain the problem clearly and separate observed facts from guesses about the cause?
- Have you followed the claim steps in the relevant document and kept copies of your submission and the company’s response?
- If the company denied coverage, have you asked for the specific written term behind its decision?

A well-kept file cannot make a limited warranty broader or guarantee a favorable decision. It can make the actual promise, the product problem and the company’s explanation easier to compare. Preserve the purchase proof, the written terms and every written clarification together; when a dispute arises, those records give you a more reliable basis for the next conversation. [S1]
