The Federal Trade Commission says it has stopped 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency’s October 29 update puts those efforts in the context of impersonation scams more broadly, which it says have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions in losses. [S1]

That update is a report on enforcement, not an announcement that every person contacted by an impostor qualifies for money. The FTC’s consumer advice also highlights a simple warning sign: anyone claiming to be from the FTC who threatens you, tells you to transfer money to “protect it,” or directs you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over is a scammer. [S1][S2]

## What changed

The FTC has publicly summarized its recent actions against imposters, including stopping 19 websites posing as the agency and reporting more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The release does not say that the $80 million is a new payment program, explain how much went to individual consumers, or identify who may qualify for any particular redress. [S1]

The announcement also comes as the FTC’s consumer-advice site flags another form of impersonation: scammers posing as well-known charities and, in some cases, telling people who have donated that they have won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. That alert, dated October 26, describes a type of pitch to watch for; it does not establish that every prize call from a charity is fraudulent. [S2]

These examples have a shared pressure point: a caller or website borrows the name of a trusted organization to make a demand or claim seem legitimate. But the FTC’s warning is especially direct about requests to move or hand over money in the agency’s name. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

If someone contacts you claiming to represent the FTC, stop before taking any financial action. A threat, an instruction to transfer money for protection, or a demand that you withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone matches the FTC’s stated warning signs. Don’t treat an official-sounding name or a convincing-looking website as proof that the contact is genuine. [S2]

If the approach is about a supposed charity sweepstakes, separate the prize claim from the organization’s name. The FTC says scammers may impersonate reputable charities and make donors believe they have won a charity-sponsored contest. The alert does not provide a test for confirming a particular prize, so don’t rely on the caller’s claim alone; pause and verify the offer through information you obtain independently rather than details supplied in the unexpected message. [S2]

A practical decision path is:

- **Is someone claiming to be from the FTC and demanding action with your money?** If they threaten you, say you must transfer money to protect it, or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over, treat the contact as a scam. [S2]
- **Is someone saying you won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes?** The FTC warns that scammers use this kind of claim to impersonate charities. Don’t let the prize claim rush you; verify the organization and the offer independently before responding. [S2]
- **Did you encounter an FTC-imposter website or another suspected fraud?** The FTC’s consumer site provides a “Report fraud” action. Reporting is a way to alert the agency; the supplied information does not promise a refund or say that a report guarantees a particular result. [S1][S2]

The redress figure is important as an account of enforcement, but it should not be confused with a personal refund notice. The release gives an aggregate amount and a count of websites stopped; it does not identify a consumer’s case, provide a payment timetable, or say that a message offering money is authentic. Treat an unexpected contact about supposed FTC redress as something to verify, not as proof that you will receive funds. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before replying, clicking, paying, or sharing information, check the exact claim and the source of the contact. For an FTC claim, compare the request with the FTC’s published warning: it says it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer your money to “protect it,” or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. If the contact asks for one of those actions, stop. [S2]

For a charity prize, verify that the charity and sweepstakes are real using contact information you find independently, not a link, number, or address supplied by the person contacting you. The FTC alert identifies charity impersonation and fake sweepstakes claims as a risk, but the supplied alert does not set out a complete prize-verification procedure or specific rules for a legitimate contest. [S2]

If you decide to report suspected fraud, use the FTC’s consumer-advice site and its “Report fraud” option. The site also lists a separate option to report identity theft, but the evidence provided does not say when a particular person should use it or what outcome to expect. [S2]

Finally, read any message about redress carefully. The FTC’s announcement reports enforcement results; it does not announce a new consumer application, individual eligibility criteria, or a deadline to claim money. If a message urges you to act quickly to receive a payment, verify it through the FTC’s official consumer information rather than relying on the message itself. [S1][S2]
