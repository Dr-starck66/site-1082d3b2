The Federal Trade Commission and officials from eight states have sued Toast Inc. and affiliated entities, alleging hidden and unauthorized fees, deceptive subscription and cancellation practices, and other unlawful conduct. The agencies also allege the restaurant software and fintech firm deceptively promoted its costs and “24/7/365” customer support. These are allegations in a lawsuit, not findings that Toast broke the law. [S1]

The announcement identifies California’s Los Angeles County Counsel and San Francisco City Attorney, as well as the attorneys general of Colorado, Florida, Illinois, Iowa, Nebraska, Oklahoma and Utah, among the plaintiffs. The supplied announcement does not provide the lawsuit’s detailed factual allegations, identify specific affected customers, or say what remedy the agencies are seeking. [S1]

That leaves an important practical distinction: the headline names fees, subscriptions and cancellation, but it does not establish that a particular Toast customer was charged improperly—or that a diner’s restaurant bill is involved. If you have an account or payment connected to Toast, check your own records before drawing conclusions from the case. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC and the state and local officials named in the announcement filed a lawsuit against Toast and affiliated entities on October 29, 2026. The agency describes the case as concerning alleged hidden and unauthorized fees, subscription and cancellation practices, and claims about pricing and customer support. [S1]

The announcement does not say whether a court has ruled on the allegations, whether Toast has agreed to resolve the case, or whether customers can seek money through it. It also does not give a filing number, describe a claims process, or announce a refund program. Consumers should not treat the lawsuit itself as proof that they qualify for a refund or other payment. [S1]

For anyone reviewing a Toast-related charge, the best first step is to identify exactly what the charge is for. Check the merchant name, date, amount and payment method against receipts, account emails and any service agreement you have. If you find a recurring charge, compare it with the subscription terms and cancellation confirmation, if available. Save copies or screenshots of the records you rely on. These steps can help you describe a concern clearly without assuming the lawsuit covers your situation.

## What this means for consumers

If you use a service billed by Toast, review the account and billing history for charges you do not recognize, repeated billing after you believe you canceled, or a price that differs from the terms you agreed to. The lawsuit announcement does not spell out which customers or transactions are at issue, so a charge that looks unfamiliar is a reason to investigate—not, by itself, proof of misconduct in your case. [S1]

Contact the company using contact information from your account or billing documents. Ask what the charge covers, when the subscription began, what cancellation terms applied, and whether the company recorded a cancellation request. If you dispute a charge or cancellation record, explain the specific amount and date, state what you expected based on your records, and keep a copy of the message and any reply. The announcement’s allegation about “24/7/365” support does not verify that support is currently available or establish how any individual request will be handled. [S1]

If the charge appears on a credit card or bank account, you can also contact the card issuer or bank to ask what options are available for that transaction. Have the statement, receipt and any correspondence ready. This is a practical escalation route, not a guarantee that a payment will be reversed; the supplied case announcement does not explain payment-dispute procedures or outcomes. [S1]

A disputed service charge is not automatically a debt-collection matter. If a collector contacts you about an amount connected to an account, the CFPB says you can use the contact to find out about the debt; a collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed and how to dispute the debt. If the collector will not or cannot provide that information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

If you are worried that an account or disputed amount has affected your credit, review your credit reports for information you do not recognize. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year and provides guidance on reviewing and disputing errors. A lawsuit announcement alone does not establish that anyone’s credit report contains an error. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Use this decision path:

- **Is the charge yours?** Match the billing entry to the merchant, service, receipt and account. If the name is unfamiliar, ask the bank or issuer and the company to identify it before assuming it relates to Toast.
- **Is it recurring or disputed?** Find the subscription terms, billing dates and any cancellation confirmation. Ask the company to explain any mismatch and keep a written record of your request and its response.
- **Has a collector contacted you?** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount and dispute instructions. The CFPB offers guidance on replying to a debt collector and disputing a debt. [S3]
- **Does your credit report show an unfamiliar item?** Review the report and use the CFPB’s credit-report guidance to learn how to dispute an error. Requesting your credit reports does not hurt your credit score, according to the CFPB. [S2]
- **Are you looking for a refund from the lawsuit?** Verify any claimed settlement, refund or enrollment instructions with an official agency source before sharing personal or payment information. The FTC announcement supplied here does not announce a refund program or consumer claims process. [S1]

The case is a reason to pay attention to billing terms and keep documentation, not a determination about any individual account. Consumers who cannot resolve a billing concern can consider contacting their bank or card issuer, and people dealing with a collection or credit-report issue can use the CFPB’s consumer resources.
