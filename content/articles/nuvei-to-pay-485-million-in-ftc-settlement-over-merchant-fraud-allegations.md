The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The FTC alleged that Nuvei and its subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement is about how a payment processor handled merchants—not a notice that every person who paid one of those merchants will receive money. The supplied FTC announcement does not identify individual consumers who qualify for compensation, explain how to apply for a refund, or say that the settlement creates a consumer payout program. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s September 4, 2026 announcement describes a settlement of its charges against Canada-based Nuvei Corporation and subsidiaries. The company will pay $4.85 million, and the announcement’s headline says Nuvei must implement robust merchant-screening practices. The extract does not spell out the specific screening procedures, implementation timeline, or how compliance will be monitored. [S1]

The underlying allegations concern Nuvei’s role in opening and maintaining payment accounts for merchants. The FTC alleged the firm knew or should have known that some merchants were engaged in deceptive activity, including tech-support scams. These are allegations settled through an agreement; the supplied text does not establish that every Nuvei merchant was deceptive or that every transaction processed by the company involved fraud. [S1]

That distinction matters if a payment statement shows a name you do not recognize. A payment processor’s name, a merchant’s name, and the name shown on a card statement may not be identical; the FTC announcement itself describes Nuvei as processing payments for merchants. The announcement does not give consumers a list of merchants involved, transaction dates, or statement descriptors to search for. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are concerned about a charge, first compare the transaction date and amount with your receipts, email confirmations, and purchases by other people authorized to use the account. This is a practical way to identify what you are investigating; it does not establish that the transaction was connected to Nuvei or to the FTC’s allegations. The announcement does not provide transaction-level details for consumers to match against their records. [S1]

If you believe you paid a scammer, the FTC consumer site points readers to its article “What To Do if You Were Scammed” and offers a way to report fraud. Use the FTC’s official consumer site rather than relying on an unsolicited message that claims to be from the agency. [S2]

Be especially wary of anyone claiming to be an FTC official who tells you to transfer money to protect it. The FTC consumer site says the agency will never threaten you, tell you that you must transfer money to protect it, or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. That warning is about impersonation scams; it is separate from the Nuvei settlement. [S2]

Do not treat the $4.85 million figure as money automatically owed to you. The source describes that amount as Nuvei’s payment to settle the FTC’s charges, but the supplied announcement extract does not explain whether any portion will be distributed to consumers or provide instructions for seeking compensation. Before sharing personal or payment information with anyone offering a “Nuvei settlement refund,” confirm the claim through an official FTC channel. [S1][S2]

A useful decision path is: identify the charge, contact the merchant using contact details you independently trust if you need clarification, and report suspected fraud through the FTC’s official site. Keep your transaction records together so you can accurately describe what happened. These are steps for organizing and reporting a concern, not a guarantee of reimbursement or a legal outcome. The FTC’s consumer site lists both fraud reporting and consumer advice as available resources. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The charge:** Check the date, amount, merchant name, and any receipt or confirmation you have. The FTC announcement does not identify particular consumer transactions or merchant descriptors related to the allegations. [S1]
- **The claim:** Look for an official FTC page before acting on a message about this settlement. The supplied sources do not announce a consumer claims process or name an administrator. [S1][S2]
- **The sender:** Do not transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold because someone claiming to represent the FTC tells you to do so; the agency says it will never make those demands. [S2]
- **The next step:** If you think you were scammed, use the FTC consumer site’s fraud-reporting option and its “What To Do if You Were Scammed” advice. The supplied extract links to those resources but does not provide their detailed instructions. [S2]

The settlement signals that merchant screening is part of the FTC’s case against Nuvei, while leaving important consumer-specific questions unanswered in the available announcement. Until official information says otherwise, treat the company payment and any possible individual recovery as separate issues. [S1]
