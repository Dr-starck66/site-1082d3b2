The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The agency alleges Nuvei opened and maintained accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement is about oversight of a company that processes payments for merchants—not a notice that a particular purchase is covered, or that individual customers will receive money. The supplied FTC announcement does not describe a consumer claim process or say that the settlement amount will be paid directly to consumers. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s September 4, 2026, announcement says Nuvei must implement robust merchant-screening practices and pay $4.85 million to resolve the agency’s charges. The complaint alleges that Nuvei and subsidiaries opened and maintained accounts for merchants involved in deceptive activity, including tech-support scams. These are allegations described by the FTC; the announcement does not provide enough detail to determine the outcome for any individual merchant or transaction. [S1]

The alleged conduct matters because consumers may pay a business without knowing which company handles the payment behind the scenes. But the FTC announcement does not say that every payment processed by Nuvei involved a scam, identify every merchant at issue, or establish that a particular transaction is connected to the allegations. A processor’s name alone is not enough to reach that conclusion. [S1]

The settlement is also not the same thing as a personal refund notice. The amount reported is the payment Nuvei will make to settle the FTC’s charges; the supplied announcement does not give consumers instructions for applying for a share or say that they qualify for one. Treat any offer claiming that this settlement guarantees you money as unverified unless you can confirm it through the FTC. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

If you are looking at a charge you do not recognize, begin with the transaction—not the settlement headline. Compare the amount and date with your own receipts, order confirmations, subscriptions, and other purchase records. Note the merchant name shown on the record and any details that might help identify the purchase. This is a way to organize what you know, not proof that a charge is legitimate or fraudulent. [S1]

If you suspect a transaction involved a deceptive merchant, write down what happened while the details are fresh: what was promised, what you paid, how you communicated with the seller, and what you received, if anything. Keep copies of relevant messages and payment records. The FTC’s announcement describes alleged merchant deception, but it does not provide individual dispute deadlines or instructions for reversing a payment. [S1]

Next, use contact information you can independently verify to ask the merchant about the charge. If you cannot identify the seller or the answer does not resolve your concern, contact the company or financial institution shown on your own payment records and ask what options apply to your transaction. This article cannot establish what options are available or how quickly you must act; the supplied sources do not state those terms. [S1]

You can also report suspected fraud to the FTC. Its consumer site provides a “Report fraud” option, and the agency’s Nuvei announcement identifies tech-support scams among the alleged deceptive activity. A report is a way to share information with the agency; the supplied materials do not promise that reporting will recover money or decide an individual payment dispute. [S1][S2]

Be cautious if someone contacts you unexpectedly and says the Nuvei settlement entitles you to a payment, then asks for money or sensitive details to release it. The FTC warns that it will never tell you to transfer money to “protect” it, or to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. Use the agency’s official site rather than a link or phone number supplied by an unsolicited message. [S2]

A practical decision path is: first, check your own records; second, ask a verifiable merchant about a charge you can identify; third, contact the payment company listed in your records if the charge remains unexplained; and fourth, report suspected fraud to the FTC. Keep the questions separate: “Is this my transaction?”, “What can I do about this payment?”, and “Should I report suspected deception?” The settlement announcement answers none of those questions for a specific consumer. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The charge:** Confirm the date, amount, merchant name, and whether your records show a purchase or recurring payment. Do not infer a connection to the FTC case from the Nuvei name alone. [S1]
- **The seller:** Check whether the merchant can explain what was sold and how the charge relates to your order. The FTC’s allegations concern certain merchants; the announcement does not name every business or transaction involved. [S1]
- **The settlement claim:** Look for an official FTC notice before responding to anyone who says you are owed money. The supplied announcement reports a $4.85 million settlement but does not provide a consumer application process. [S1][S2]
- **The next contact:** Use details on records you already trust, and ask the payment company what process and timing apply to your situation. The supplied sources do not set a deadline for an individual complaint or payment dispute. [S1]
- **The escalation:** If you believe you encountered fraud, use the FTC’s official “Report fraud” channel. Do not treat filing a report as a guarantee of repayment. [S2]
