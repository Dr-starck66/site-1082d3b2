The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and adopt stronger merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The agency alleges Nuvei opened and maintained accounts for businesses it knew or should have known were deceiving customers, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement raises a practical question: if you paid a business that later seemed fraudulent, does this settlement mean you can claim money back? The information provided does not identify an individual refund program, a claims process, or eligibility rules. Treat the settlement as an FTC enforcement action—not as a promise of a payment to any particular customer. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC announced the settlement on September 4, 2026. Under the agreement described in its announcement, Nuvei is to pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant screening practices. The agency’s charges concern Nuvei and its subsidiaries processing payments for merchants allegedly engaged in deception. [S1]

The distinction between an allegation and an established finding matters. The FTC says the merchants were businesses Nuvei “knew or should have known” were engaged in deception; the supplied announcement describes the case as charges being settled. It does not provide enough detail here to identify every merchant involved, list specific screening requirements, or explain what evidence the FTC relied on for each charge. [S1]

The agency specifically points to tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. That makes the case relevant to people who paid for supposed technical help, but the announcement excerpt does not name affected consumers or say that every tech-support payment processed by Nuvei was fraudulent. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

A payment processor can appear in the chain between a customer’s payment and a merchant. So a processor’s name on a transaction may not, by itself, tell you which business sold you something or whether your particular payment is connected to the FTC’s allegations. Start with the transaction details you can verify: the amount, date, any merchant or descriptor name, and records of what you thought you were buying. The FTC’s case identifies Nuvei’s alleged role in processing payments for merchants; it does not identify individual transactions. [S1]

Use this decision path:

- **You don’t recognize a charge:** Compare the statement entry with receipts, order confirmations, subscriptions, and any purchase made by another account user. Note the exact descriptor and amount before deciding what the charge relates to.
- **You recognize the merchant but suspect deception:** Gather what was promised, what you received, and your communications with the business. The FTC’s announcement describes allegations about merchants’ deceptive activity, but it does not say that every dispute with a merchant falls within this case. [S1]
- **The purchase involved supposed tech support:** Check whether the service and business match what you were told, and keep the payment and service records together. The FTC says the alleged merchant fraud included tech-support scams; it does not give a list of covered businesses or transactions. [S1]
- **You paid someone you think was a scammer:** The FTC’s consumer-advice site lists guidance titled “What To Do if You Were Scammed,” including situations where someone paid a suspected scammer or shared personal information or access to a device. Use that guidance for next steps rather than assuming this settlement will resolve your individual case. [S2]

If you contact a company or agency about your own payment, describe what happened and distinguish what you observed from what you suspect. Keep copies of statements, receipts, messages, and any response you receive. These records can help you explain the transaction; they do not establish that you qualify for money from the settlement. The FTC announcement supplied here does not describe any consumer eligibility or distribution process. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before treating a notice, call, or online post as an offer of settlement money, check whether it identifies an official FTC program and points to details the agency has actually announced. The supplied FTC announcement reports Nuvei’s settlement payment and screening obligations, but does not state that consumers should pay a fee, share account credentials, or submit a claim to receive funds. Be cautious about anyone using the case to promise a refund or request sensitive information. [S1]

For your own transaction, verify the merchant descriptor against your records, the date and amount, and what the seller said it would provide. If you suspect a scam, consult the FTC’s consumer advice for people who paid someone they think was a scammer. If the facts don’t match the Nuvei case, don’t force the connection: the announcement concerns FTC charges against a payment processor and alleged merchant conduct, not a general ruling on every disputed payment. [S1][S2]

The useful takeaway is limited but important: the FTC says Nuvei must strengthen how it screens merchants, following allegations that it processed payments for deceptive businesses. Consumers with a suspicious transaction still need to assess their own records and use consumer-protection guidance; the settlement announcement alone does not establish a personal refund right or say how to obtain one. [S1][S2]
