A payment processor can sit between a merchant and a customer’s payment, but the latest FTC action is aimed at the processor’s relationships with merchants—not a new refund program for consumers. The Federal Trade Commission says Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and implement stronger merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. [S1]

For consumers, the practical distinction matters: the agency’s announcement describes an enforcement settlement, but the available details do not provide instructions for claiming money or say that consumers will receive individual payments. If you are concerned about a charge, a credit-report entry or a collection notice, first identify which of those issues you actually have; they call for different next steps. [S1][S2][S3]

## What changed

The FTC says global payment processor Nuvei and its subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants that the company knew or should have known were engaged in deception. The agency specifically cites tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. These are allegations in an FTC complaint; the company agreed to settle the charges, and the FTC announcement says the settlement requires a $4.85 million payment and robust merchant-screening practices. [S1]

That makes merchant screening the central issue in the announcement. The FTC’s claim is not simply that some consumers encountered deceptive sellers; it alleges that the processor continued to provide payment services to merchants despite warning signs or knowledge of deceptive activity. The settlement’s screening requirement is intended to address the processor’s role in handling merchants, rather than to give consumers a new way to dispute a particular charge. [S1]

The headline amount is the payment Nuvei is to make to settle the FTC’s charges. The information available here does not describe it as a per-person refund, set out a consumer application process or give a deadline to file a claim. Consumers should not assume that a settlement payment automatically means they are owed a share. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you think a payment was connected to a tech-support scam, start with the transaction itself: note the date and amount, identify the merchant shown on your receipt or account, and preserve messages, invoices and other records. The FTC’s announcement establishes that its complaint involved deceptive merchants and tech-support scams, but it does not identify individual customers or transactions. Matching your own records to the specific merchant is therefore more useful than relying on the Nuvei name alone. [S1]

Next, separate a payment concern from a credit-report concern. If you are worried an account or other information is appearing on your credit file, review your credit reports for unfamiliar or inaccurate entries. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year, says requesting them will not hurt your credit score, and points consumers to guidance on disputing errors. [S2]

A disputed payment and a credit-report error are not the same issue. The CFPB’s credit-report resources explain how to review and dispute report information; they do not say that the Nuvei settlement will remove an entry or resolve a payment dispute. Keep copies of the records related to each issue and follow the process that applies to the specific company or report involved. [S1][S2]

If the problem instead involves someone trying to collect a debt, do not assume that a payment processor’s settlement has erased or confirmed that debt. The CFPB says a debt collector should provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed and information about how to dispute the debt. If the collector will not or cannot provide that information, the CFPB says it could be a scam; its debt-collection resources explain how to reply and how to seek more information. [S3]

The order of action is straightforward: document the transaction or notice, decide whether the issue is a charge, a credit-report entry or a collection attempt, then use the relevant channel. The FTC’s announcement includes a route to report fraud, while the CFPB provides separate resources for credit-report errors and debt-collection questions. A report or dispute is a way to raise an issue; the available sources do not promise a refund or a particular result. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **Check the merchant and transaction details.** Compare the name, amount and date on your statement or receipt with any messages or records you kept. The FTC announcement discusses merchants generally and does not list individual consumer transactions. [S1]
- **Confirm whether you have a credit-report issue.** Review the report itself before assuming a suspicious payment changed your credit file. The CFPB says consumers can request reports without hurting their scores and offers guidance on disputing errors. [S2]
- **If contacted about a debt, ask for the required details.** Confirm the creditor, amount and instructions for disputing it before deciding what to do. The CFPB says a collector is required to provide that information. [S3]
- **Look for actual claim instructions before expecting settlement money.** The available FTC announcement describes Nuvei’s $4.85 million settlement payment and screening requirements, but does not provide an individual claim form or deadline. Do not treat the settlement amount as a promised consumer payout. [S1]
- **Keep the channels separate.** A fraud report, credit-report dispute and response to a debt collector address different problems. Use the channel that matches the evidence you have, and retain copies of what you submit. The FTC and CFPB provide separate resources for these issues. [S1][S2][S3]
