A payment processor is not usually the business a shopper thinks they are paying. But when a processor keeps handling transactions for merchants accused of deception, it can become part of a consumer-protection case. The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei and subsidiaries opened and maintained accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deceptive activity, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle the FTC’s charges and must implement robust merchant-screening practices. [S1]

For consumers who may have paid a questionable seller, the practical point is narrower than the headline: this is an enforcement settlement involving Nuvei’s role as a processor, not an announcement that each affected customer will receive money. The supplied FTC announcement does not describe individual payments to consumers, a claims process, or a deadline to apply. Don’t treat the $4.85 million figure as a promised refund or assume you qualify for any payment. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s complaint alleges that Nuvei Corporation and its subsidiaries processed payments for merchants engaged in deception. The agency says the companies knew or should have known about that activity. The announcement specifically mentions tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers; it does not identify those consumers or list every merchant involved in the allegations. [S1]

Nuvei agreed to pay $4.85 million to settle the FTC charges and to implement robust practices for screening merchants. That puts the focus not only on what the merchants allegedly did, but also on the processor’s decisions about which businesses could use its payment services and whether to continue processing for them. [S1]

A settlement is not the same as a consumer-facing refund notice. In the material supplied here, the FTC does not say the settlement fund will be distributed to customers, explain how to file a claim, or state that a person who encountered a tech-support scam is eligible for money. Consumers should wait for a specific official notice before sharing personal or financial information in response to claims about a Nuvei-related payout. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are trying to work out whether a charge relates to the kind of conduct described by the FTC, start with what you can verify: the date, amount, merchant name shown on your statement, and any receipt, email, website, or support-session details connected with the purchase. Compare records rather than relying on a caller, text, or online post that simply mentions Nuvei. The FTC announcement describes Nuvei as a payment processor and alleges activity by merchants; it does not provide a list of consumer-facing merchant names. [S1]

If you recognize a suspicious tech-support payment, make a short timeline while the details are fresh: how the seller contacted you, what it promised, what you were asked to buy, and how you paid. Keep copies of receipts and messages. These records can help you explain what happened when you contact the seller or the company that handled your payment. This is a practical recordkeeping step, not a guarantee of reimbursement or a statement about your legal rights. [S1]

Next, use contact information you can independently verify—for example, details on a statement or the company’s official website—to ask the merchant or your payment provider what options may be available for your particular transaction. Explain the facts and ask whether there is a process to dispute or report the charge. Do not assume the Nuvei settlement changes the terms, deadlines, or outcome for an individual payment; none of those details appears in the supplied announcement. [S1]

Be especially cautious if someone says you must pay a fee, transfer money, or provide account information to release a share of the settlement. The FTC’s consumer site lists reporting fraud as an available action and has an article titled “What To Do if You Were Scammed,” which covers situations where someone paid a suspected scammer or shared personal information or access to a device. Use official FTC pages reached directly, rather than links from an unexpected message. [S2]

If you are unsure whether the issue is the merchant, the payment itself, or a claim about compensation, separate those questions. First document and report the transaction through a channel you can verify. Then check whether the FTC has published a specific consumer notice about payments connected to this case. A company’s agreement to pay a settlement does not by itself confirm that a particular shopper is covered. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Check the exact charge, date, merchant name, and receipt. The FTC announcement does not provide a merchant list for consumers to match against. [S1]
- **The claim about money:** Look for an official FTC notice that identifies who may receive funds, how to apply, and any deadline. The supplied announcement gives the settlement amount but no consumer-claim instructions. [S1]
- **The person contacting you:** Independently find the organization’s official contact details. Do not rely on a message’s link or on someone’s assertion that the settlement requires an upfront payment. [S2]
- **Your next channel:** Ask the merchant or payment provider what options apply to your transaction, and keep a record of the response. The FTC announcement does not specify an individual dispute process or guarantee an outcome. [S1]

The takeaway is to treat the FTC action as a warning about alleged merchant-screening failures and a reason to check records—not as proof that every Nuvei-processed purchase was fraudulent or that consumers will automatically be paid. Verify your own transaction, use official contact channels, and act only on instructions that an agency or payment company has actually published. [S1][S2]
