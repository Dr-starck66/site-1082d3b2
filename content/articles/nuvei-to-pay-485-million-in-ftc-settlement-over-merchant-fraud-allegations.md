A payment processor may be unfamiliar to shoppers, but it can sit between a merchant and a consumer’s payment. The Federal Trade Commission says Nuvei opened and maintained processing accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle the FTC’s charges and must implement robust merchant screening practices. [S1]

The announcement is significant, but it does not establish that every consumer who paid a merchant using Nuvei was harmed, or that the settlement will send money directly to individual customers. The evidence provided does not describe a consumer claims process, refund eligibility, or an application deadline. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC announced the settlement on September 4, 2026. It alleges that Canada-based Nuvei Corporation and its subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants involved in deceptive activity, including tech support scams. The agency says those scams took millions of dollars from consumers. These are allegations the company is settling, not a reason to assume that every merchant using the processor acted fraudulently. [S1]

Under the settlement described by the FTC, Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant screening practices. Screening requirements address the processor’s handling of merchants; they do not, based on the supplied announcement, tell an individual customer whether a particular charge is refundable or how to request money. [S1]

That distinction matters if you recognize Nuvei’s name on a payment record. The announcement concerns the processor and its alleged relationship with merchants; it does not identify a list of affected merchants, transactions, or consumers. A processor name alone therefore cannot confirm that a charge was connected to the conduct described by the FTC. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are checking a charge, start with the transaction rather than the processor’s name. Compare the amount and date with receipts, emails, order confirmations, and any service agreement you have. Write down the merchant name shown on the transaction and what you believe you paid for. This is a practical way to separate a charge you recognize from one you need to investigate; it is not a determination of fraud or refund eligibility.

If a charge is unfamiliar, or if a merchant’s service differs from what you were told, contact the business using contact information you can independently verify and ask for an explanation and records of the purchase. Keep copies of your correspondence and payment records. The FTC’s consumer advice site provides a “What To Do if You Were Scammed” article for people who paid someone they think may be a scammer, as well as a way to report fraud. [S2]

If you decide to report what happened, describe the transaction and the merchant’s conduct plainly. Include dates, amounts, receipts, messages, and what you have already done to resolve the issue, if available. The FTC lists reporting fraud as a consumer action, but the supplied information does not promise a response, a refund, or a particular outcome from a report. [S2]

Do not treat the $4.85 million figure as money automatically available to customers. The FTC announcement says Nuvei will pay that amount to settle its charges, but the supplied material does not say that consumers can claim a share or explain how any consumer payment would be distributed. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Confirm the date, amount, and merchant name using your own payment records. A processor’s appearance in the FTC announcement does not, by itself, identify your transaction as part of the alleged conduct. [S1]
- **The merchant’s promise:** Review what you were told you would receive and what, if anything, you actually received. Preserve the relevant receipt, messages, and service details so your account is specific and supportable.
- **The route for a concern:** Check the FTC’s consumer advice resources for its article on what to do if you were scammed and its fraud-reporting option. The supplied page links to both; it does not set out a deadline or guarantee an individual recovery. [S2]
- **Any claim about settlement money:** Verify it against an official FTC notice before sharing personal or payment information. The provided settlement announcement does not describe a consumer claims program, so claims that it guarantees you a payment are not supported by this evidence. [S1]

The practical decision path is straightforward: if the transaction is recognized and matches what you agreed to buy, keep the records; if it is unclear, ask the merchant for an explanation; if you suspect deception or cannot resolve the issue, consult the FTC’s consumer advice and consider reporting the facts. The settlement raises scrutiny of Nuvei’s merchant screening, but it does not replace checking your own transaction or establish a refund entitlement. [S1][S2]
