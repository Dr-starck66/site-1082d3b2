Payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million to settle Federal Trade Commission charges that it facilitated merchant fraud, including tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers, the agency announced Sept. 4. The FTC alleged that Nuvei and its subsidiaries opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement is a significant enforcement action against a payment processor—but the supplied FTC release does not identify individual merchants or explain a process for consumers to claim money from the settlement. It also does not say that every payment handled by Nuvei involved fraud. A charge against a processor is not, by itself, proof that a particular transaction was fraudulent or that a consumer is owed a refund. [S1]

## What changed

Nuvei must pay $4.85 million to resolve the FTC’s charges and implement robust merchant screening practices, according to the agency’s announcement. The FTC said its case concerned Nuvei and subsidiaries processing payments for merchants engaged in deception, including tech support scams. [S1]

The complaint’s central allegation is about what Nuvei knew or should have known when it opened and maintained merchant accounts. That is different from a finding that every customer who paid a merchant using Nuvei was defrauded, or that Nuvei itself sold the product or service. The supplied announcement describes a settlement of FTC charges; it does not provide details about the screening requirements or name the merchants involved. [S1]

The $4.85 million is the amount Nuvei will pay to settle the charges. The FTC material provided here does not say that this amount will be distributed directly to consumers, set out a consumer claim form, or give a deadline to apply. Don’t treat the settlement figure as a promised payment to customers. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you recognize a payment that may relate to a tech support scam or another deceptive merchant, start with the transaction record rather than the processor’s name. Check the date, amount, merchant name as it appears on your account, and any receipt, email, or service record. Keep copies of those details so you can explain what happened clearly. The FTC announcement does not list specific merchants, so it cannot confirm from the information provided whether a particular charge is connected to the case. [S1]

If you believe a charge was unauthorized or tied to a service you did not receive as promised, contact your bank or card issuer and ask what options and procedures apply to your transaction. The supplied sources do not establish a dispute deadline or guarantee that a bank will reverse a payment, so ask directly and promptly rather than relying on the settlement amount as a remedy. [S1]

A suspicious payment and a credit-report problem are separate issues. The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year for errors that could affect a credit score, loan terms, or access to credit; it also explains how consumers can review and dispute inaccurate information. If the payment issue appears alongside an unfamiliar account or other credit-report error, review the report and follow the relevant dispute steps. [S2]

If someone later contacts you claiming you owe money connected to a transaction, don’t assume the debt is valid just because a collector says so. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, amount owed, and information on how to dispute the debt; if the collector will not or cannot provide this information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

## What to verify before you act

Use this decision path before sharing information or making another payment:

- **Check the transaction.** Match the charge to your bank or card statement and any receipt or correspondence. The FTC announcement does not identify the merchants, so a processor name alone cannot establish that your charge is part of the case. [S1]
- **Ask your payment provider what applies.** Contact the bank or card issuer using the contact information on its official website or your card, describe why you are questioning the charge, and ask what documentation and timing apply. The supplied sources do not state a universal deadline or guarantee a reversal. [S1]
- **Treat settlement claims cautiously.** The FTC’s announcement says Nuvei will pay $4.85 million, but the supplied material does not describe consumer payments or a claims process. Verify any purported settlement notice with the FTC before providing sensitive information or paying a fee. [S1]
- **Check related records.** If you suspect the incident affected your credit history, review your credit reports and dispute information you believe is inaccurate. Requesting your credit reports will not hurt your credit score, according to the CFPB. [S2]
- **Verify any debt separately.** If contacted by a collector, ask for the creditor, amount, and dispute instructions before paying or sharing information. The CFPB says these details are required and that a refusal or inability to provide them could signal a scam. [S3]

Keep the evidence you gather, including account statements and messages, and note whom you contacted and when. These steps can help you explain the issue to your provider or distinguish an actual transaction from a suspicious follow-up, but they do not guarantee a refund or other outcome. [S1][S3]
