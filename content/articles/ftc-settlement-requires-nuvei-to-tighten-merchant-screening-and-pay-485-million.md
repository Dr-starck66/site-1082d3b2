## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says payment processor Nuvei will pay $4.85 million and must adopt robust merchant-screening practices to settle charges that it facilitated merchant fraud. The FTC alleged that Nuvei opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaging in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. Those are allegations described in the agency’s announcement, not a claim that every Nuvei transaction or merchant was fraudulent. [S1]

The case focuses on a part of a payment that most shoppers rarely see. A merchant sells a product or service; a payment processor helps that merchant accept payments. The FTC’s allegation is that Nuvei’s role went beyond simply handling payments because it continued processing for merchants that should have raised concerns. The settlement’s screening requirement addresses that alleged gap. [S1]

The announcement identifies the $4.85 million as payment to settle the FTC’s charges, but the supplied text does not describe individual consumer payments, a claims process, or who might qualify for money. Consumers should not treat the company’s settlement amount as a promised refund or assume that a particular charge is covered. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

For a person who paid a suspicious merchant, the case is a reason to look closely at the transaction—not proof that Nuvei handled it or that the purchase was fraudulent. The FTC announcement concerns allegations involving merchant accounts and deceptive activity; it does not identify individual customers or transactions. Start with what you can confirm: the merchant name, the date and amount, what you expected to receive, and any emails, receipts, or other records tied to the purchase. [S1]

Use those details to decide what to do next. If the transaction matches something you knowingly bought and received, the settlement alone does not change what the FTC announcement says about your purchase. If the merchant or product is unfamiliar, or the service you paid for was not what was represented, preserve your records and contact the business using contact information you can independently verify. This is a practical way to investigate a disputed purchase; the supplied announcement does not set out a consumer refund procedure. [S1]

If you believe you encountered fraud, the FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report it. A report gives the agency information about your experience; the supplied sources do not promise that reporting will recover your money or produce a particular result. Keep a copy of what you submit and the records that support it. [S2]

Be cautious if someone contacts you claiming that the Nuvei settlement guarantees you money. The FTC’s consumer site warns that the agency will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to protect it, or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. Those are warning signs the agency specifically flags; do not follow such instructions. [S2]

The practical takeaway is to separate three questions: Do you recognize the transaction? Do your records support a concern about what the merchant promised or delivered? And are you being asked to take an unusual step—such as transferring money—to receive supposed settlement funds? Your answers can help you decide whether to contact the merchant, report a suspected scam, or pause and verify a claim before sharing money or information. The FTC announcement does not provide a case-specific consumer payout route. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **Match the charge to your records.** Check the amount, date, merchant name, receipt, and any order or service details before concluding that the transaction relates to the FTC case. The announcement does not name individual customers or purchases. [S1]
- **Check the claim, not just the caller.** If someone says you are owed money, look for confirmation in official FTC information rather than relying on an unsolicited message. The announcement supplied here does not explain a consumer claims process. [S1]
- **Stop if you are told to send money to get money.** The FTC says it will not threaten you or tell you to transfer funds, withdraw cash, or buy gold and give it to someone. [S2]
- **Choose an escalation based on what you can establish.** For a purchase concern, keep supporting records and contact the merchant through independently verified details. For suspected fraud, the FTC consumer site offers a reporting route; neither source promises a refund or a specific outcome. [S1][S2]

The settlement matters because it requires Nuvei to strengthen how it screens merchants and includes a $4.85 million payment, while the allegations describe processing for merchants accused of deception. For an individual consumer, the next step is still evidence-led: verify the transaction, retain records, and use the FTC’s reporting option if you believe you were scammed—without assuming the settlement itself entitles you to a payment.
