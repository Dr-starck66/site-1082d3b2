## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. Those figures show the scope of the agency’s response, but they do not mean every person who has encountered an imposter is owed money or qualifies for a payment. [S1]

The FTC described its work as part of ongoing efforts to fight impersonation scams. The agency’s consumer-protection director said imposter scams had been the top reported fraud category over several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That statement describes reports overall; it does not establish how likely any individual is to lose money or recover it. [S1]

The agency’s latest consumer guidance also points to scams that borrow trust from familiar organizations. A caller might claim to represent a charity and say that someone who donated has won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. In a separate alert, the FTC warned about imposter ads appearing in social-media feeds, search results and shopping platforms, and said it is exploring additional ways to protect consumers from them. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

An official-looking website, ad, message or caller ID is not enough to establish that a contact is genuine. The FTC specifically warns that scammers use ads to impersonate businesses and government agencies, and says an imposter may try to make a charity supporter believe they have won a prize. Treat an unexpected request to act as a reason to pause—not as proof that the organization named is involved. [S2]

The clearest warning sign in the FTC’s guidance is a demand to move or hand over money in the name of protecting it. The agency says it will never threaten you, tell you that you must transfer your money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. If a person claiming to be from the FTC makes one of those demands, do not follow the instructions. [S2]

A practical decision path is to stop the interaction first. Don’t click through the suspicious ad or use contact details supplied in an unexpected message or call. Instead, look up the organization through a channel you find independently, and ask whether it sent the notice or ran the promotion. For a supposed sweepstakes, check whether you entered and whether the charity can confirm it through its own known contact channel; the FTC’s alert identifies charity-linked prize claims as a possible impersonation tactic. [S2]

If the contact is an ad, preserve enough information to describe where you saw it, what organization it claimed to represent and what it asked you to do. The FTC says it wants to hear from consumers about imposter ads and is exploring ways to address them. You can also use the FTC’s report-fraud option; its consumer site separately lists reporting identity theft and getting a free credit report as available actions. [S2]

The FTC’s enforcement figures are agency-level results, not a promise of compensation to everyone affected. The announcement reports more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the rule took effect, but the supplied information does not identify a particular payment program, who qualifies for one, or how to apply. Don’t pay a supposed recovery agent or share sensitive information just because someone claims the FTC has money waiting for you. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a suspicious contact, check these points:

- **What are they asking for?** A threat, a demand to transfer money to protect it, or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over conflict with the FTC’s warning about impersonators. Stop rather than comply. [S2]
- **Did you expect the contact?** For a claimed charity prize, consider whether you entered a sweepstakes and verify the claim with the charity using contact information you locate yourself. The FTC warns that scammers may connect a prize claim to a charity you support. [S2]
- **Where did you see the offer?** An ad in a social-media feed, search results or a shopping platform can still be an imposter ad. Don’t treat placement or an organization’s name as verification. [S2]
- **Are they promising you money?** The FTC’s reported redress total concerns enforcement actions; the supplied announcement does not say that a specific consumer is eligible for funds. Verify any claimed payment through official FTC information, not through the person who contacted you. [S1]
- **What happened already?** If you shared information or believe you paid a scammer, the FTC consumer site lists reporting fraud and identity theft among its actions. It also offers information on getting a free credit report. Use the option that fits what happened, and consult the FTC’s consumer guidance for next steps. [S2]

When in doubt, end the conversation and verify separately before sharing information or sending money. Report suspicious imposter ads or fraud through the FTC’s consumer resources; the agency says it is seeking consumer input on imposter advertising. [S2]
