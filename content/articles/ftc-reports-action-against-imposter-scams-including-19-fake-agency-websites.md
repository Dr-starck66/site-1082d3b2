The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The update is a reminder that scammers may borrow the name of a trusted agency or business to make a demand seem legitimate—but a headline about enforcement does not mean every person who reports a scam will receive money back. [S1]

For consumers, the most useful takeaway is a decision rule: if someone claiming to represent the FTC tells you to move money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over, stop. The FTC says it will never make those demands. [S2]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, announcement, the FTC highlighted its ongoing efforts against impersonation scams. It reported that 19 websites posing as the FTC had been removed and that enforcement actions had resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the agency’s Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

The agency also described the scale of the problem: its Bureau of Consumer Protection director said imposter scams had been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That describes reports across consumers; it does not establish how much any one person lost or what that person may recover. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer guidance makes the warning concrete. Someone pretending to be the agency may use its name to make a frightening or urgent request, but the FTC says it will not threaten you, instruct you to transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. [S2]

Impersonation is not limited to government agencies. The FTC’s consumer site warns that scammers may pose as small businesses, including around Small Business Saturday, to get consumers to pay them. A familiar business name or a timely shopping pitch, on its own, does not establish that the person contacting you represents that business. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Start with what the person wants you to do—not how official the caller, message, or website looks. A demand to transfer money for protection, hand over cash or gold, or act under threat conflicts with the FTC’s stated guidance about how it operates. End the interaction rather than following the instructions, and do not use contact details supplied in the suspicious message to check whether it is real. [S2]

Next, separate a report about enforcement from a personal refund offer. The FTC’s announcement gives an aggregate figure for consumer redress from enforcement actions; it does not say that everyone who lost money to an imposter qualifies, that payments are automatic, or that a particular caller can secure a payment. Treat an unexpected promise of money tied to this announcement as something to verify, not as proof that the offer is genuine. [S1]

If the impersonator claims to represent a business, pause before paying. Check whether the business and the specific request match information you can find independently, rather than relying on a link, phone number, or payment instructions the sender provided. The FTC’s warning about small-business impersonators makes the distinction important: a real business can be copied by someone trying to collect money. [S2]

If you have already acted, avoid letting embarrassment or urgency push you into another payment. The FTC consumer site provides a way to report fraud and links to advice on what to do if you were scammed. Use those resources to identify appropriate next steps for what happened; the available guidance does not promise that a report will recover money. [S2]

A practical order of operations is: stop the demanded payment; preserve the message and details of what happened; check the claim through a channel you find independently; then use the FTC’s report-fraud and consumer-advice resources if you believe you were targeted. This sequence applies whether the claimed identity is the FTC or a business, while keeping the key distinction clear: a warning sign justifies caution, but it does not by itself determine what happened or what remedy is available. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

- **Who contacted you?** Check the identity through contact information you locate independently, not details supplied by the person making the demand. [S2]
- **What are they asking for?** A threat or instruction to transfer money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over is a clear reason to stop. [S2]
- **Are they promising redress?** The FTC’s $80 million figure concerns enforcement actions overall; verify any claim that you personally qualify before sharing information or paying a fee. The announcement does not establish individual eligibility. [S1]
- **What happened already?** If you paid or shared information, consult the FTC’s consumer advice and report-fraud resources for next steps rather than relying on a stranger’s promise to fix it. [S2]
