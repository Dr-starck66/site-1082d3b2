The Federal Trade Commission says it has stopped 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also says imposter scams have been the most-reported fraud category for several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

Those figures describe agency actions and reported losses—not a promise that an individual who encounters a suspicious message will receive money back. For consumers facing a supposed debt collector, or worrying that a fraudster may have used their identity, the practical first step is to pause and verify the claim through reliable channels. [S1][S2][S3]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026, announcement, the FTC highlighted its ongoing efforts against impersonation scams. It reported removing 19 FTC-imposter websites and said enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

The announcement does not say that every reported scam has been stopped, that all affected consumers have been identified, or that more money is available to claim. It also does not provide details in the supplied information about how consumers can qualify for any particular redress program. Treat a message promising a payment as something to verify independently, not as proof that you are owed money. [S1]

The FTC’s figures arrive against a large reported problem: the agency says imposter scams have ranked as the top reported fraud category over the past several years and consumers have reported billions in losses. Reported losses are not necessarily a measure of every scam or every consumer’s individual experience. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a caller, email, text, or website says it represents the FTC, a creditor, or a debt collector, don’t let urgency substitute for verification. The supplied FTC announcement confirms that FTC-impersonation websites have been targeted, but it does not describe every tactic scammers use. Avoid assuming that a familiar-looking name or an official-sounding claim proves who contacted you. [S1]

If the contact is about a debt, use the interaction to establish what is being collected and whether you owe it. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, how to dispute the debt, and other information. If the collector will not or cannot provide it, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

A useful decision path is: first, don’t pay or share information just because a message creates pressure; second, ask the collector for the debt information the CFPB says it must provide; third, check the claim before deciding whether to dispute it or take another step. The CFPB advises consumers to find out about a debt and determine whether it is really theirs. [S3]

If you suspect someone used your identity or that a fraudster’s actions affected your credit, review your credit reports for information you don’t recognize. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year for errors that could lower a score or affect access to credit or loan terms. It also says requesting your reports will not hurt your credit score. [S2]

A report review is a way to spot and act on possible problems, not proof by itself of who caused them. The CFPB points consumers to resources for disputing a credit-report error and lists identity theft as a situation consumers may need to address. Keep notes about unfamiliar entries and use the relevant reporting company’s process to review or dispute them. [S2]

Consumers can also choose an escalation route that fits the issue: the FTC’s site offers a way to report fraud, while the CFPB offers a complaint process and information about debt collection and credit reports. A report or complaint is a way to alert an agency; the supplied material does not promise a particular investigation, resolution, or payment. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **For a claimed debt:** Ask for the creditor’s name, the amount, and how to dispute it. If that information is missing or the collector cannot provide it, treat the claim cautiously and verify before paying. [S3]
- **For a message claiming to be from the FTC:** Don’t rely on a link or contact detail in the message to establish its legitimacy. Go to the FTC’s official website independently to find its fraud-reporting option. The agency’s announcement specifically reports stopping websites that impersonated the FTC. [S1]
- **For a promised refund or redress payment:** Verify whether the FTC has published information about the specific matter and who may qualify. The announcement’s figure of more than $80 million does not establish that you personally qualify for a payment. [S1]
- **For possible credit-file problems:** Review your reports and use the CFPB’s guidance on disputing errors if you find information that is wrong or unfamiliar. [S2]
- **For unresolved concerns:** Consider submitting a complaint to the CFPB about a consumer-finance issue or reporting suspected fraud to the FTC. Keep a record of what you were told and what you submitted; neither agency’s listed resources guarantee a particular outcome. [S1][S2][S3]
