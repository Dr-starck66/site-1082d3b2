## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission highlighted its ongoing efforts to combat impersonation scams. The agency said it had removed 19 websites impersonating the FTC and that enforcement actions had resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The announcement describes agency activity; it does not say that every person contacted by a scammer qualifies for redress or that money is currently available to claim. [S1]

The FTC also described impersonation scams as a leading reported fraud category in recent years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That context matters for people dealing with credit or debt: a convincing message that claims to come from a government agency, creditor, or collector can create pressure to act before checking who is actually on the other end. [S1]

The update is not a new instruction for consumers to pay a debt, provide personal information, or apply for a payment. Nor does the supplied announcement identify individual recipients, a claim process, or a deadline for getting redress. Treat any message making those claims as something to verify independently, rather than as proof that the FTC has contacted you. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If someone contacts you about a debt, separate two questions: Is the debt yours, and is the person contacting you legitimate? The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information about how to dispute the debt, and other details. If the person will not or cannot provide the information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

Use the information to check the claim before deciding what to do. Compare the creditor and amount with records you already have, and ask how to dispute the debt if you do not recognize it. The CFPB advises using contact with a collector to learn about the debt and determine whether you owe it; it also says you should tell the collector if you believe you do not owe it. [S3]

A caller’s confidence, a familiar logo, or a website that looks official is not enough to establish identity. The FTC’s announcement confirms that websites impersonating the agency have been removed, while the CFPB warns that a collector unable or unwilling to provide key debt information could be fraudulent. As a practical safeguard, don’t use links or phone numbers supplied in a suspicious message to verify the sender; find the agency or company’s contact details independently. [S1][S3]

If the issue involves your credit file, check the reports themselves rather than relying only on what a caller says. The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year for errors that could lower a score or affect access to credit or loan terms. It says requesting your credit reports will not hurt your score and provides a list of credit reporting companies where consumers can request and review reports. [S2]

If you find an unfamiliar account or other information that looks wrong, the CFPB offers a guide on disputing a credit report error. If you suspect identity theft, its credit-report resources identify fraud alerts and security freezes as protections to explore. These steps address your records; they do not, on their own, establish who sent a message or resolve whether a claimed debt is valid. [S2]

A useful decision path is: first, pause and verify the sender; second, request and review the debt details; third, dispute a debt you believe you do not owe; and fourth, review your credit reports if the contact suggests an account or identity issue. If a collector continues to contact you or you need help understanding your options, the CFPB lists guidance on replying to collectors and submitting a complaint. The FTC also provides a way to report fraud. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **Who is contacting you?** Independently locate the organization’s official contact information and check whether the message matches what that organization says. The FTC says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency, so a government-looking site alone is not confirmation. [S1]
- **What debt is being claimed?** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount, and how to dispute the debt. If the collector will not or cannot provide those details, the CFPB says the contact could be a scam. [S3]
- **Does your credit report support the claim?** Review your reports for unfamiliar or incorrect information. The CFPB recommends checking them at least annually and explains how to request reports and dispute errors. [S2]
- **Is a promised payment actually described by the agency?** The FTC update reports aggregate redress from enforcement actions, but the supplied announcement does not give a recipient list, application link, or deadline. Verify any specific offer with the FTC through contact information you find independently. [S1]

For debt-collection questions, the CFPB lists its consumer contact number as 855-411-2372 and provides an online complaint option. The FTC’s site offers a fraud-reporting option. Keep copies of messages and notes about contacts so you can explain what happened when seeking help. [S1][S3]
