A payment processor can sit behind a transaction without being the name a consumer recognizes. That can make it hard to connect a charge to the company that handled the payment. The Federal Trade Commission says Nuvei opened and maintained accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were engaged in deception, including tech-support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

The FTC announced a settlement requiring Nuvei to pay $4.85 million and implement robust merchant-screening practices. The case concerns the processor’s alleged role in enabling merchant fraud; the announcement does not say that consumers will receive payments from the settlement. [S1]

## What changed

The FTC’s September 4, 2026 announcement describes charges against Canada-based Nuvei Corporation and its subsidiaries. The agency alleges they opened and maintained payment-processing accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were deceiving consumers. The settlement calls for Nuvei to pay $4.85 million and adopt stronger merchant screening. [S1]

The allegations include merchants behind tech-support scams that, according to the FTC, took millions of dollars from consumers. That is an allegation in the FTC’s case, not a finding about every Nuvei transaction or every merchant using a payment processor. The announcement does not identify individual consumers or list specific transactions. [S1]

For consumers, the practical change described is aimed at how Nuvei screens merchants—not a new payment option or a change to consumers’ credit-report rights. The supplied announcement does not spell out the screening procedures, say when each measure takes effect, or describe a process for consumers to claim money. It would be misleading to treat the $4.85 million figure as a promised refund pool for Nuvei customers. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you are checking a transaction, start with the records you control: review card and bank statements for charges you do not recognize, note the date and amount, and save any receipt, email, or message connected to the purchase. A processor may not be the merchant name shown on a statement, and the FTC announcement does not provide a list of affected merchants or charges. [S1]

If the charge appears connected to a service you never requested, such as a purported tech-support service, contact your bank or card issuer using the number on your card or its official website and ask what options apply to that transaction. Keep a record of the conversation and any case number. The evidence provided here does not specify dispute deadlines or guarantee that a charge will be reversed, so check the issuer’s instructions promptly rather than assuming the settlement changes those procedures.

If a charge leads to a claimed debt or a contact from a debt collector, first ask for details that help you determine whether the debt is yours. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt, among other information; inability or refusal to provide it could indicate a scam. [S3]

If you suspect your information was used fraudulently, consider checking your credit reports for unfamiliar entries. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year for errors that could affect a score, credit eligibility, or loan terms, and says requesting reports does not hurt your credit score. If you find an error, the CFPB provides a guide to disputing it. [S2]

You can also use the FTC’s fraud-reporting channel or the CFPB’s complaint channel to share a concern. Those agencies provide reporting and complaint options, but the materials supplied do not promise that a report will produce a refund or resolve an individual dispute. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Before treating a charge, debt, or credit entry as part of this case, check:

- **The transaction details:** Match the date, amount, and merchant description against receipts, emails, and account records. The FTC announcement does not name individual merchants or transactions. [S1]
- **The company involved:** Ask your card issuer or bank whether it can identify the merchant behind an unfamiliar processor description. Do not assume that a Nuvei connection, by itself, proves fraud or makes a transaction eligible for a settlement payment. [S1]
- **Any claimed debt:** Request the creditor’s name, amount, and instructions for disputing it, and compare those details with your own records. [S3]
- **The credit record:** If you suspect fraud has affected your credit file, review your reports and use the CFPB’s dispute guidance if you find an error. [S2]
- **The settlement’s scope:** Confirm any consumer-payment or claim information through an official FTC notice. The supplied announcement says Nuvei will pay $4.85 million but does not describe direct payments to consumers or a claim process. [S1]

The key decision is whether you have a specific charge, debt, or credit-report entry to address. If so, follow the relevant bank, collector, or credit-reporting process and keep your documentation. If you have no such issue, the FTC announcement alone is not evidence that you are owed money or that your accounts were affected.
