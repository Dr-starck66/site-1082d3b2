A payment processor may be part of a purchase even when its name is not the one a customer recognizes. That distinction is central to the Federal Trade Commission’s settlement with Nuvei: the agency says the processor opened and maintained accounts for merchants it knew or should have known were deceiving consumers, including tech support scammers. Nuvei must pay $4.85 million and implement merchant-screening practices under the settlement announced September 4, 2026. [S1]

The FTC’s announcement describes charges and allegations, not a finding that every merchant using Nuvei was fraudulent or that every customer who paid one is entitled to money back. It also does not announce a direct payment program for affected consumers. The practical next step depends on what happened to your money: a questionable charge, an unfamiliar debt, or a credit-report entry each calls for a different response. [S1][S2][S3]

## What changed

The FTC alleged that Canada-based Nuvei Corporation and its subsidiaries opened and kept payment-processing accounts for merchants engaged in deception. The agency said Nuvei knew or should have known about the conduct, which included tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. [S1]

Nuvei agreed to settle the FTC’s charges by paying $4.85 million and implementing robust merchant-screening practices. The settlement addresses the processor’s screening of merchants; the supplied announcement does not say that the payment will be distributed to consumers or that people who paid a merchant will automatically receive reimbursement. [S1]

That makes this a case about how a payment company handles the businesses it serves, rather than a notice that consumers should expect a check. The FTC’s summary does not identify a claims form, enrollment process, or consumer payment deadline. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you suspect you paid a deceptive tech-support seller, start with the transaction rather than the processor name. Check your card or bank statement for the merchant name, amount, and date; save receipts, emails, messages, and any description of what was promised. These records can help you explain the problem when you contact the financial institution that handled the payment. The FTC announcement does not provide a specific dispute deadline or guarantee an outcome, so contact that institution promptly and ask what options apply to your transaction. [S1]

If you see an unfamiliar charge, don’t assume it came from Nuvei just because the FTC case concerns a payment processor. The announcement does not list individual merchants or identify consumers whose transactions were processed by Nuvei. Ask your card issuer or bank to help identify the transaction and explain its process for reporting an unauthorized or disputed payment. [S1]

If the experience involved a debt collector rather than an active payment, ask for the information needed to identify and assess the debt. The CFPB says a collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt, among other information. If the collector will not or cannot provide that information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

If you’re concerned that the incident affected your credit history, check your credit reports for unfamiliar accounts or inaccurate information. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year and says requesting them will not hurt your credit score. If you find an error, the bureau provides guidance on disputing it; a credit report review is a separate step from challenging a card or bank transaction. [S2]

A useful decision path is: contact your bank or card issuer about a payment; ask a collector for debt details if someone is pursuing a balance; and review your credit reports if you see a possible credit-file problem. Keep each issue’s records together, and do not treat the Nuvei settlement itself as proof that a specific charge, debt, or credit entry is connected to the case. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **For a payment:** Confirm the merchant name and transaction details on your statement. Ask your financial institution how to report the charge and what information it needs. The supplied FTC announcement does not set a consumer deadline or promise reimbursement. [S1]
- **For a debt:** Ask the collector for the creditor’s name, amount, and dispute instructions before deciding what to do. The CFPB recommends using the contact to find out whether the debt is yours. [S3]
- **For a credit-report concern:** Review your reports and follow the CFPB’s guidance for disputing errors. Requesting your reports does not hurt your score, according to the CFPB. [S2]
- **For a possible scam:** Preserve messages and transaction records, and don’t rely on an unsolicited caller’s claim that the Nuvei settlement guarantees you money. The FTC announcement supplied here describes a payment to the government, not a consumer claims program. [S1]

Keep the questions distinct: Was the payment yours? Is a collector asking for a debt you recognize? Does a report contain an error? The answers determine which organization to contact and what to ask; the settlement alone does not resolve an individual consumer’s case. [S1][S2][S3]
