The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency announced the figures on Oct. 29, 2026, as part of an update on its efforts to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The FTC also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That signals a persistent problem, but the agency’s figures do not tell an individual consumer whether a particular call, message or website is legitimate. [S1]

For people managing credit or debt, the practical challenge is deciding what to do when a message claims to come from an agency, creditor or debt collector. A convincing name or logo is not proof. Before paying, sharing personal information or acting on pressure, pause and verify the claim through a channel you find independently. [S1][S3]

## What changed

The FTC’s update describes ongoing enforcement against impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 websites posing as the FTC. The agency also reported more than $80 million in consumer redress tied to enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. These are reported agency-wide results, not a promise that a person who has lost money will receive a payment. [S1]

The announcement puts the work in context: according to the FTC, consumers have reported billions of dollars in losses to imposter scams, which have been the top reported fraud category over several years. The release does not provide, in the supplied information, a breakdown by scam type or details about how an individual redress case is handled. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Treat an unexpected request for money or personal information as a reason to check—not as proof that the supposed agency or company is contacting you. If someone claims you owe a debt, the CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information on how to dispute the debt, and more. If the person will not or cannot provide this information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

Use a simple decision path: first, don’t let urgency push you into paying or disclosing information on the spot. Next, ask for the debt details and review them. Then independently look up the organization’s contact information and reach out through that route—not through contact details supplied in a suspicious message. The CFPB’s debt-collection guidance can help you understand what to ask and how to reply. [S3]

If the caller says the debt is yours, that alone does not settle the question. The CFPB advises consumers to use the contact as an opportunity to find out about the debt and determine whether they really owe it. If you believe you do not owe it, the CFPB says you should tell the debt collector; it also offers guidance on disputing a debt. [S3]

If you suspect fraud or identity theft, review your credit reports for information that does not look right. The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year, says checking them will not hurt your credit score, and points consumers to resources for requesting reports and disputing errors. A report review is a way to spot potential problems; it does not by itself establish who caused them or resolve a dispute. [S2]

Keep records of the contact and any documents or payment requests, and use official consumer-protection channels to seek help. The FTC provides a way to report fraud, while the CFPB accepts complaints and offers debt-collection guidance. Reporting a suspicious contact does not guarantee a refund or a particular result. [S1][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **The debt:** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount owed and information on how to dispute it. Compare the details with your own records; don’t assume the claim is accurate just because the contact sounds official. [S3]
- **The sender:** Check the organization using contact information you locate independently. The FTC’s announcement confirms that websites impersonating the agency have been removed, so a site’s appearance alone should not be your only check. [S1]
- **The next step:** If you think you owe the debt, use the CFPB’s guidance to understand how to communicate with a collector. If you think you do not owe it, tell the collector and look into disputing it. If you suspect identity theft or see an unfamiliar item on your credit report, use the CFPB’s report-review and error-dispute resources. [S2][S3]
- **Any promised recovery:** The FTC’s reported redress is an aggregate result of enforcement actions. Verify any claim that you are personally owed money rather than treating the agency’s total as evidence of an individual payment. [S1]
