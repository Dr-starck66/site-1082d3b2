## What changed

A federal court has permanently banned operators of a debt-relief operation that the Federal Trade Commission said targeted seniors and veterans by impersonating banks, credit card companies and government agencies. The FTC announced the court’s action on October 22, 2026. [S1]

The court entered a default judgment of $106.8 million against corporate defendants on October 14 and ordered them to turn over assets, according to the FTC. The release identifies Accelerated Debt Settlement Inc., ADS Resolve LLC and Financial Solutions Group LLC among the defendants. [S1]

The case concerns alleged impersonation and an alleged debt-relief scheme. The announcement says the judgment was entered by default; it does not describe a consumer refund process, say that payments will be made to affected people, or explain how to claim money. Consumers should not treat the judgment amount as a promised payout. [S1]

That distinction matters if you are contacted by someone claiming to represent the court, the FTC or one of the businesses and offering to release funds. The supplied FTC announcement confirms a judgment and asset-turnover order, but it does not announce a payment program or provide instructions for receiving a refund. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If you paid a company that promised debt relief, start by separating what you know from what the company claimed. Save contracts, payment records, emails, texts, letters and the names or numbers used by the people who contacted you. Those records can help you describe what happened if you seek help; they do not establish that you qualify for a refund or other recovery. [S1]

If someone contacts you about a debt, ask for information that lets you identify it and decide whether you owe it. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed and how to dispute the debt, among other information. If the person will not or cannot provide the information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]

Do not assume a debt-relief company and a debt collector are the same thing. The CFPB describes a debt collector as someone who may be contacting you because a creditor believes you are past due. For a collector’s claim, focus first on the debt details and whether you owe it; for a company’s offer to reduce or settle debt, verify who is making the offer and what it says it will do before sharing information or paying. [S3]

If you suspect that a scheme involved your personal or financial information, review your credit reports for information you do not recognize. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year and says you have the right to see and act on data in your report. Its resources also cover what to do if you think you may have been a victim of fraud or identity theft. [S2]

Use a decision path rather than relying on the caller’s urgency: if a person claims you owe a debt, request the debt information; if you do not recognize the debt or the information is missing, do not treat the claim as verified; if you paid a debt-relief business, organize your records and check for an official update before responding to anyone promising a payment. The CFPB provides guidance on replying to collectors, disputing credit-report errors and understanding debt-settlement options. [S2][S3]

If a collector continues contacting you and you want the contact to stop, the CFPB says you have the right to tell a debt collector to stop contacting you. The agency also points consumers to guidance on disputing a debt and on what to do if a creditor or collector sues. Use the appropriate route for the problem rather than assuming that a request to stop contact resolves whether the debt is owed. [S3]

The CFPB’s resources can help consumers understand the next step, but the agency says its consumer information is not legal advice. If you are unsure how to respond to a lawsuit or how the court’s order affects your situation, consider seeking qualified legal help rather than relying on a company’s sales pitch or a stranger’s interpretation. [S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **Who is contacting you?** Independently check the person’s claimed organization and role. The FTC announcement describes alleged impersonation of banks, credit card companies and government agencies; a familiar name or official-sounding claim is not proof of affiliation. [S1]
- **What is the specific debt?** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount and dispute instructions. If those details are not provided, the CFPB says the situation could be a scam. [S3]
- **What does the court announcement actually say?** It reports a default judgment and an order to turn over assets, but the supplied announcement does not describe a consumer payout or a process for claiming one. [S1]
- **Is your credit information accurate?** Review your reports for unfamiliar entries and use the CFPB’s credit-report resources to learn how to dispute errors. [S2]

Keep records of any contacts and do not pay or disclose information based only on a promise tied to this case. Verify the claim first, then use CFPB guidance or qualified help suited to your situation. [S1][S2][S3]
