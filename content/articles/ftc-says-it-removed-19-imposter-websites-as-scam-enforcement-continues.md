## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement describes ongoing efforts; it does not say that every person contacted by an impostor is eligible for money or that a new refund program is open. [S1]

The FTC also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That is a warning about the scale of reported harm, not a measure of any one person’s risk or proof that a particular caller, email, or website is fraudulent. [S1]

The headline numbers concern agency action against impersonation. They do not, on their own, establish what happened in an individual case, whether a consumer’s information was exposed, or whether the consumer’s credit file has changed. Those questions call for separate checks. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

An official-looking name or website is not enough to establish that a message is genuine. If someone claiming to represent the FTC asks you to pay, share account details, or take an urgent step, pause rather than relying on the contact information or link in that message. Look up the organization independently before responding. The FTC’s announcement confirms that websites have impersonated the agency, but it does not provide a complete list of scam tactics or a test that can authenticate a specific message. [S1]

If the contact is about a debt, handle it as a verification problem before deciding whether to pay. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, how to dispute the debt, and other information. If the person will not or cannot provide that information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. Ask for the details, keep a record of the contact, and use the CFPB’s debt-collection guidance to understand how to respond. [S3]

A real debt and a fake collector are different possibilities: a suspicious call does not establish that you owe nothing, and a collector’s claim does not establish that the debt is yours. The CFPB advises consumers to use the contact to find out about the debt and determine whether they owe it. If you believe you do not owe it, tell the collector; the CFPB also provides guidance on replying and disputing a debt. [S3]

If you suspect that an imposter has used your personal information, check your credit reports for unfamiliar information. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least once a year for errors that could lower a score or affect access to credit or loan terms. It says requesting your reports will not hurt your credit score, and offers a list of credit reporting companies for requesting and reviewing reports. [S2]

Use a simple decision path: pause the contact; verify who is contacting you and what the request concerns; if it is debt-related, ask for the creditor, amount, and dispute information; then check your reports if you suspect identity misuse. Keep messages, letters, and dates together so you can explain what happened if you report the contact or dispute information. The verification steps are especially useful when a message mixes a claim about debt with pressure to act quickly. [S2][S3]

If you find an error on a credit report, the CFPB provides a guide for disputing it. If a collector’s contact is the issue, consult the CFPB’s debt-collection resources; the agency also accepts consumer complaints. For suspected fraud, the FTC’s site provides a way to report fraud. Reporting or disputing an issue is a next step, not a guarantee of a particular outcome. [S1][S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **Who contacted you?** Check the organization through a source you find independently, rather than treating an official-looking name or link as proof. The FTC says imposters have created websites posing as the agency. [S1]
- **What is the request?** Before paying or sharing information, make sure you understand what is being claimed and why. If the request concerns a debt, ask for the creditor’s name, amount, and information about how to dispute it. [S3]
- **Does the debt belong to you?** Compare the information provided with your own records. The CFPB advises using the contact to determine whether you owe the debt; it says missing required information could indicate a scam. [S3]
- **Does your credit file show a problem?** Review your reports for unfamiliar information, and use the CFPB’s dispute guide if you find an error. The CFPB recommends checking reports at least annually. [S2]
- **Where can you escalate?** The FTC offers fraud reporting, and the CFPB has debt-collection resources and a consumer complaint channel. Gather the message and relevant records before reporting or asking for help. [S1][S3]

The FTC’s figures describe enforcement activity, not an individual consumer’s eligibility for redress. Verify any claim of a payment or official case through independently located agency information, and do not treat a promise of money as proof that the contact is legitimate. [S1]
