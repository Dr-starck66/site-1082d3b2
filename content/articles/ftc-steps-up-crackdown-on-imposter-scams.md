## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission highlighted continuing efforts against impersonation scams. The agency said it stopped 19 websites that impersonated the FTC and obtained more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. [S1]

The update does not describe a general FTC payment program or establish a way for every recipient to claim money. Instead, it summarizes enforcement by the agency against businesses or scammers operating through impersonation tactics. [S1]

Imposters have been the FTC’s top-reported fraud category for several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That scale explains why a message, call, or website that looks official deserves more scrutiny than the agency name alone. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

An impersonation scam does not need to copy the FTC perfectly. It can claim to represent the FTC, another federal agency, a government benefits office, a bank, a utility, or a company you know. The person may use official-looking names, logos, case numbers, badges, or urgent instructions. The FTC has made clear that its own representatives will not threaten people or demand that they move money to protect it. [S1][S2]

The FTC also says it will never tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. Treat that demand as a warning sign, whether it arrives by phone, text, email, or through a website. [S2]

**Use this decision path before responding.**

**1. Stop the requested action.** Do not send cash, buy prepaid cards or cryptocurrency, transfer funds, or purchase gold merely because someone says the action is required by the FTC or another authority. The FTC has specifically identified a demand to move money for protection as a scam warning sign. [S1][S2]

**2. Verify the sender through a separate route.** Open a known agency or company address yourself rather than following a link or dialling a number from the message. Federal government websites should end in **.gov**, and a secure page should begin with **https://**. These signals do not prove that an offer is legitimate, but they give you a stronger starting point than a caller or search result. [S2]

**3. Identify what the contact is asking for.** A request for money, account details, passwords, verification codes, or remote access should trigger extra caution. The FTC’s consumer advice separates common threats into categories such as unwanted calls and texts, government and business impersonators, identity theft, and online security. [S2]

**4. Match the message to the agency’s public process.** If someone claims the FTC has taken an enforcement action involving you, do not rely on the message to establish its authenticity. Look for the agency through its official site and use only a contact method you locate independently. [S1][S2]

**5. Escalate the report or exposure.** If you lost money or disclosed personal information, use the FTC’s consumer advice site to locate the appropriate next step. The site provides links to report fraud and report identity theft. [S2]

### Common situations

**You receive a warning that your bank account will be frozen.** Stop before making a transfer. A real institution may have legitimate account protections, but the safest way to check is to sign in through your bank’s own app or known website, or call a number listed on a card or statement. [S2]

**Someone says the FTC is protecting your money.** The FTC will not contact you to tell you to transfer money to a supposed safe account. It will not instruct you to keep moving funds through cash, gold, gift cards, cryptocurrency, or other unusual methods. [S1][S2]

**A caller provides a case or badge number.** An official-sounding detail does not verify identity. Close the call and initiate contact independently through a known agency or organization. [S2]

**A search result leads to a hard-to-find store or agency.** The FTC advises going directly to a company’s website when it is possible to do so. This reduces reliance on look-alike stores, sponsored search results, or copied pages. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, transferring, investing, sharing data, granting device access, or confirming an identity, verify these points:

- **Who initiated the contact:** Did you choose the channel, or did someone arrive through an unexpected call, text, email, pop-up, or social-media message? [S2]
- **What the person is demanding:** The FTC does not threaten people or require money to be moved for “protection.” [S1][S2]
- **How payment is requested:** A request to withdraw cash or buy gold for an individual is a prominent warning sign. [S2]
- **Where the official page was found:** Check the .gov status of a federal agency page and confirm the https connection, then navigate independently rather than using embedded links. [S2]
- **What information is already being requested:** Do not provide passwords, account credentials, security codes, Social Security information, or remote-device access unless you initiated contact and can verify the recipient through an independent source. [S2]
- **Whether the event matches an FTC enforcement announcement:** A general press release may report a case or redress effort without giving you a right to an individual payment. [S1]

After a suspected scam, preserve the original message and report it through the appropriate FTC channel. If the issue involves fraud, use the fraud-reporting link; if someone obtained or used personal information, use the identity-theft reporting link. The FTC’s consumer advice page identifies both reporting options. [S2]

For any active financial or account loss, stop further transfers and contact the relevant financial institution using information you already have from its card, statement, app, or official website. This can help separate a possible impersonation report from the institution’s specific account procedures. [S2]

The immediate goal is not to prove whether every unknown contact is fraudulent. It is to prevent payment or information transfer until the person and demand have been checked through a separate, reliable route. [S1][S2]
