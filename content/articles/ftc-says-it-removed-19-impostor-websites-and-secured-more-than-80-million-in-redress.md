The Federal Trade Commission says it has stopped 19 websites that impersonated the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also says impostor scams have been the most-reported fraud category for several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

That announcement shows the scale of the FTC’s enforcement response, but it does not mean every person targeted will receive money or that every message claiming to be from the agency is genuine. The practical test is what the contact asks you to do: the FTC warns that it will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or ask you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S1][S2]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026 announcement, the FTC highlighted its efforts against impostor scams, including the removal of 19 websites posing as the agency. It also reported more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

The FTC described impostor scams as the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions in losses. Those figures describe reports and agency-wide enforcement results; they do not establish that a particular consumer lost money, that every reported loss was recovered, or that someone who receives a message is eligible for redress. [S1]

The announcement is therefore useful as a warning and an enforcement update, not as a notice that consumers should expect a payment. The supplied information gives no individual claim process, payment deadline, or eligibility criteria for the redress figure. Treat any unsolicited message that promises you money because of the announcement as unverified. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If someone claiming to represent the FTC contacts you, focus first on whether they demand action with your money. A threat, an instruction to transfer funds to keep them safe, or a request to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over is a scam warning sign identified by the FTC. Do not follow that instruction. [S2]

Use a simple decision path:

- **They demand a transfer, cash withdrawal, or gold handoff:** stop. The FTC says it will not make those demands to protect your money. [S2]
- **They threaten you:** stop. The FTC says it will not threaten you. [S2]
- **They mention an enforcement action or redress:** do not assume the contact is authentic or that you qualify. The FTC’s announcement reports an aggregate result, not a payment determination for you. [S1]
- **You are unsure what to do next:** use the FTC’s consumer advice site and its “Report fraud” option rather than relying on instructions in the unexpected contact. [S2]

The difference between enforcement and an individual remedy matters. The FTC says its actions resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress, but the announcement extract does not explain who received that redress or how to determine whether a specific person qualifies. A caller or message that uses the figure to pressure you into paying a fee, sharing information, or acting immediately is not verified by the announcement. [S1]

Keep the response narrow if the contact is suspicious: do not send money or hand over valuables because of the demands listed above, and do not treat a claimed FTC identity as proof. Instead, consult the agency’s consumer advice resources or report the suspected fraud through the FTC’s report-fraud option. The supplied material confirms those resources are available, but does not specify what information a report must include or what outcome to expect. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message about the FTC or a supposed redress payment, check these points:

- **What exactly are you being asked to do?** A threat or request to transfer money to “protect it,” withdraw cash, or buy and hand over gold conflicts with the FTC’s warning. [S2]
- **Is the promised money tied to a specific process?** The announcement provides no individual eligibility rules, claim deadline, or instructions for receiving redress. Do not infer those details from the headline figure. [S1]
- **Can you reach the agency’s consumer information independently?** The FTC’s consumer advice site offers a report-fraud option. Use that resource to seek guidance rather than following an unexpected contact’s directions. [S2]
- **Are you being rushed or threatened?** The FTC says it will not threaten you. Pause instead of acting on pressure. [S2]

The FTC’s figures document enforcement and reported harm; they are not a guarantee of recovery for any individual. When a supposed agency contact asks you to move money or valuables, the safest next step supported by the FTC’s warning is not to comply, then use the agency’s consumer advice and fraud-reporting resources.
