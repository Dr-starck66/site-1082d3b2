## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency highlighted the efforts in an October 29 announcement about its work to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

That figure is a report of outcomes from enforcement actions, not a promise that an individual who lost money will receive a payment. The announcement does not say that all scam losses are covered or that a consumer can qualify simply by reporting a scam. [S1]

The FTC also described impersonation scams as part of a persistent problem: its Bureau of Consumer Protection director said they had been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

A contact that claims to be from the FTC deserves special scrutiny, especially if it pressures you to move money. The agency’s consumer advice says the FTC will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. Those demands are a scam warning sign. [S2]

Use a simple decision path before taking action:

- **If someone claims to be an FTC official and demands money, stop.** Do not transfer funds, withdraw cash, buy gold, or give valuables to the caller or a person they send. The FTC says it does not make those demands. [S2]
- **If the contact says there is an urgent problem, verify before responding.** Don’t rely on a phone number, link, or instructions supplied in the message. Instead, find the agency’s official website independently and use its published contact or reporting options. The FTC’s consumer site provides scam advice and links to report fraud. [S2]
- **If you already paid or shared personal information, use the FTC’s reporting options.** The FTC consumer site lists separate options to report fraud and identity theft, and links to advice about what to do after a scam. A report is a way to alert the agency; the supplied information does not promise that reporting will recover money. [S2]

The agency’s $80 million-plus redress figure may be encouraging, but it is tied to enforcement actions, not a general reimbursement program. Don’t trust anyone who claims they can secure an FTC refund for a fee or guarantees a payout based on that announcement; the cited release does not describe any such service or guarantee. [S1]

Scammers may invoke a real agency or use a convincing-looking website. The FTC’s announcement that it removed 19 impersonation websites is a reminder that a familiar name or polished page alone is not enough to establish that a contact is genuine. [S1]

## What to verify before you act

Before you send money, provide personal information, or follow instructions from someone claiming to represent the government, check these points:

- **What are they asking you to do?** A threat, a demand to transfer money for protection, or an instruction to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over is inconsistent with the FTC’s stated practices. End the interaction rather than trying to negotiate. [S2]
- **Who initiated the contact?** Don’t treat an unsolicited call, message, or website as verified because it uses an agency’s name. Locate the agency’s official site on your own and use its available resources to check the claim. [S1][S2]
- **Are they promising a refund?** The FTC’s announcement reports more than $80 million in redress from enforcement actions, but it does not establish that you are owed money or that a third party can obtain it for you. Verify any claim through the FTC’s own consumer resources, and be wary of demands for payment to release a supposed refund. [S1][S2]
- **Have you already acted?** Go to the FTC consumer site directly and choose the reporting option that matches what happened—fraud or identity theft. Keep any relevant messages and payment details available when you consult the agency’s advice; don’t assume that filing a report guarantees a particular outcome. [S2]

The practical takeaway is straightforward: treat a demand to move or hand over money as a stop signal, verify agency claims through official resources you locate yourself, and report suspicious activity through the FTC’s own channels. The FTC’s enforcement figures describe agency action, not an individual consumer’s eligibility for redress. [S1][S2]
