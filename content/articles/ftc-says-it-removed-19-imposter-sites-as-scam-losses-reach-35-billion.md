The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites pretending to be the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The update comes as the FTC warns consumers about fake agency websites and other imposter scams. [S1][S2]

The numbers show that impersonation is a significant enforcement focus, but they are not a promise that a person who has lost money will receive it back. The announcement describes agency-wide actions; it does not identify a new payment program or say that every scam victim qualifies for redress. [S1]

## What changed

In an October 29 update, the FTC highlighted its efforts to combat impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 FTC-imposter websites and enforcement actions resulting in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The agency said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions in losses. [S1]

A November 12 FTC consumer alert added context: the agency said it received more than 1 million reports about imposter scams last year, with reported losses of $3.5 billion. The alert specifically cautioned about people and websites pretending to be the FTC. [S2]

Those figures describe reports and enforcement results, not a count of every scam or a forecast of what any individual may recover. The supplied FTC announcement does not provide case-by-case eligibility details, a deadline to seek redress, or instructions for applying for a payment. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

If a message or caller claims to represent the FTC, start with the demand—not the logo, caller ID, or website design. The FTC says it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or instruct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. Treat any such demand as a scam. [S2]

If you’re unsure, stop before paying, sharing information, or following a link in the message. Check the agency’s website independently rather than using the contact details or web address provided by the person who contacted you. The FTC’s site explains that official federal websites use .gov addresses and that a secure connection begins with https://; those signs help verify a site address, but they do not prove that an unexpected demand is legitimate. [S1][S2]

Use a simple decision path:

- **They threaten you or demand a transfer, cash, or gold:** Don’t comply. The FTC identifies those demands as signs of an imposter scam. [S2]
- **They send a link or claim a website is the FTC:** Don’t rely on the link. Independently check the address for the official .gov domain and secure https:// connection described by the FTC. [S1]
- **You still can’t verify the claim:** Pause rather than deciding under pressure. The FTC’s consumer site provides a way to report fraud and publishes consumer alerts. [S2]
- **You already paid or shared information:** The FTC lists “What To Do if You Were Scammed” among its consumer advice articles and provides a report-fraud option. Use those official resources to determine what steps fit your situation; the supplied material does not specify a universal recovery process. [S2]

The enforcement announcement is useful as a signal that the agency is acting against impersonation, not as evidence that a particular caller or recovery offer is connected to that work. A person claiming they can get you FTC redress still needs to be assessed independently—especially if they ask you to send money or move funds. The FTC’s warning about transfer demands applies regardless of the claim used to justify them. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message that invokes the FTC, check these points:

- **What exactly are they asking you to do?** A threat, a demand to transfer money to protect it, or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone are red flags identified by the FTC. [S2]
- **Are you on an official site?** Type or independently locate the agency’s address instead of opening a link sent by a stranger. Check for the .gov domain and https:// connection noted on the FTC site. [S1]
- **Are they promising a payment or recovery?** The FTC’s reported $80 million-plus in consumer redress covers enforcement actions; the announcement does not say that all victims qualify or describe an open application process. Don’t treat the headline figure as an individual entitlement. [S1]
- **Where can you escalate a concern?** The FTC’s consumer site offers a report-fraud option and consumer alerts. For a suspected imposter, use those official resources rather than the contact information in the suspicious message. [S2]

The safest next step depends on what happened: reject coercive payment demands, verify the web address independently, and use the FTC’s own reporting and advice pages if you suspect an imposter. The agency’s enforcement figures provide context, but they cannot verify a specific contact or guarantee a financial outcome. [S1][S2]
