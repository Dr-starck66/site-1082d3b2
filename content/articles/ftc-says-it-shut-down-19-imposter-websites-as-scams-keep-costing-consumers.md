## What changed

On October 29, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission highlighted its efforts against impersonation scams. The agency said it had removed 19 websites impersonating the FTC and that enforcement actions had resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement describes enforcement results; it does not say that every person who was targeted or lost money will receive a payment. [S1]

The FTC said imposter scams had been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That is a statement about reported fraud and losses, not a measure of how much money any one person can recover. [S1]

For consumers, the announcement is a reminder to pause when a message or website claims to represent a government agency, a company, or a debt collector. The supplied FTC announcement does not provide a list of the removed websites or describe specific scam scripts, so consumers should not treat it as a way to identify every current fake site or message. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a message says you owe money, will be penalized, or can receive money if you act quickly, separate the claim from the requested action. Don’t use a link or phone number in the message as your only way to verify it. Instead, look up the agency or company independently and compare the request with its official information. The FTC’s site provides a way to report fraud, but its announcement does not promise that reporting will recover money. [S1]

If the person says they are collecting a debt, ask for information that lets you check the claim. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information on how to dispute the debt, and more. The CFPB says that if the collector will not or cannot provide this information, it could be a scam. [S3]

Use the information to make a decision, not to rush into payment. The CFPB advises using contact with a debt collector to find out about the debt and determine whether you really owe it. If you do not believe the debt is yours, the bureau says you should tell the collector; its site also links to guidance on replying to a collector and disputing a debt. [S3]

If you suspect someone used your identity or financial information, check your credit reports for unfamiliar entries. The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year, says requesting them will not hurt your credit score, and notes that its list of credit reporting companies can help you request and review reports. [S2]

A practical order of operations is: stop and verify who contacted you; request and review the debt details if the contact concerns a collection; then check your credit reports if you suspect identity misuse or see an unfamiliar account. Keep notes about what you were told and what you checked so you can explain the issue if you contact an agency or credit reporting company. The CFPB provides paths to dispute credit report errors and submit a complaint, though neither source promises a particular outcome. [S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **Who is contacting you?** Find the organization through an independent search or its official website rather than relying only on a message’s link or callback number. The FTC’s announcement confirms that FTC-imposter websites have been removed, but it does not name them. [S1]
- **What exactly is being claimed?** For a debt collection contact, check the creditor’s name, the amount, and how to dispute the debt. If the person will not or cannot provide the requested information, the CFPB says it could be a scam. [S3]
- **Does your credit file support the claim?** Review your reports for unfamiliar information and use the CFPB’s credit reporting company list to request them. Requesting a report will not hurt your credit score, according to the CFPB. [S2]
- **Where can you escalate?** The FTC offers a fraud-reporting option, and the CFPB offers a complaint process and guidance on debt collection and credit report disputes. These are ways to report or seek guidance—not guarantees of a refund, correction, or other result. [S1][S2][S3]

Before sharing sensitive information or sending money, verify the request through a channel you found independently. If you already responded, use the FTC’s fraud-reporting option or the CFPB resources that match the issue, and check your credit reports if identity misuse is a concern. The available sources do not specify a deadline for reporting these incidents, so act promptly without relying on an unverified caller’s instructions. [S1][S2][S3]
