## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission said on October 29, 2026 that it removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and highlighted enforcement actions that have produced more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. [S1]

The release describes imposter scams as the top reported fraud category over several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. The FTC said it intends to continue vigorous enforcement against deceptive impersonation schemes. [S1]

This is an enforcement update, not a promise that a particular consumer will receive a refund or that every unpaid or disputed debt is fraudulent. The announcement does not list individual eligibility rules or a claim deadline for any redress program. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

Imposter scams can create pressure around money in several ways: a caller says the FTC or another government agency has a refund for you, an online seller claims to represent a debt collector, or a message warns that a fee will resolve an account or prevent an arrest. Treat the identity of the sender and the urgency of the request as separate issues. [S1]

That distinction matters when a real debt also appears. An actual balance does not become automatically valid just because a caller identifies the creditor accurately, and a false claim does not become valid merely because it includes an official-sounding name or government seal.

For a possible collection issue, first decide whether the person is genuinely trying to collect a debt or is using that claim to obtain money or personal information. A debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and instructions for disputing the debt, among other information. A collector that will not provide those details could be operating a scam. [S3]

Do not respond to a payment demand until you compare the explanation with the records you already have. Use the original creditor or a known account statement to check the balance rather than account numbers, links or phone numbers supplied by the caller.

### Use this decision path

**If the contact says it is from the FTC or another government agency:**  
- Do not send money or sensitive information immediately. [S1]
- Independently find the agency’s official contact channel and initiate a call or visit yourself. [S1]
- Be especially cautious if the person insists on gift cards, cryptocurrency or a same-day payment; the FTC release identifies impersonation activity but does not establish that those methods are legitimate government payment instructions. [S1]

**If the contact names a credit account or debt:**  
- Ask for the creditor’s name, total amount, and written dispute instructions. [S3]
- Review whether the debt belongs to you by comparing it with account statements, credit reports and records of payments. [S2][S3]
- If you believe you do not owe the debt, tell the collector. Avoiding or ignoring a collector is unlikely to make it stop. [S3]

**If the contact mentions fraud or identity theft:**  
- Check your credit reports for unfamiliar accounts or unexplained changes. The CFPB recommends checking credit reports at least once a year so errors or signs of fraud can be identified early. [S2]
- Use the reports to determine whether additional action is needed; requesting credit reports does not itself hurt a credit score. [S2]
- Preserve messages, call logs and payment records before reporting or disputing the matter. [S1][S2]

**If you already paid:**  
- Document the amount, payment method, date and recipient. [S1]
- Stop additional transfers or purchases instead of paying escalating fees. [S1]
- Report the incident through an official fraud channel and retain the confirmation. [S1]

A legitimate debt can coexist with a deceptive collection tactic. Conversely, a fraudulent offer can use an accurate-looking account number or a familiar creditor name. The FTC’s enforcement action does not replace the need to verify the specific transaction or account.

## What to verify before you act

Before you pay, disclose information or close an account, verify the following:

1. **Who is asking?** Confirm the organization independently, using a number or website you located yourself rather than contact details supplied by the message. [S1]
2. **What debt or refund is involved?** Match the name, account, amount and creditor against written records you control. [S2][S3]
3. **What proof is available?** Ask for the information a debt collector is required to provide, including the creditor’s name, amount owed and how to dispute the debt. [S3]
4. **What will happen to the money?** Verify the payee and payment method through the legitimate organization or your financial provider before authorizing a transfer. [S1]
5. **Does the offer depend on a deadline or fee?** The FTC release does not provide a deadline or eligibility rule for its redress actions, so treat any promised date or fee as something to verify rather than a fact. [S1]

## How to respond and escalate

Start with the least risky step: do not send information or money while the claim is unresolved. If the contact concerns a debt, seek the collector’s details and compare them with the original creditor. If you do not owe the debt or need proof, the CFPB directs consumers to dispute the debt or use the available guidance on communicating with collectors. [S3]

Check your credit reports if the situation could affect your accounts or credit record. The CFPB recommends requesting reports and reviewing unfamiliar information, especially if you suspect identity theft or fraud. [S2]

For a report or complaint, use official channels: the FTC advises consumers to report fraud, and the CFPB provides a consumer complaint process. Keep copies of what you submit and any reference numbers. [S1][S2]

If an account may be affected, contact the financial institution through its known customer-service channel. Explain that the communication is being verified and ask what security steps, disputes or account restrictions the institution can document. The evidence here supports reviewing reports and disputing errors but does not specify a universal resolution or outcome for every account. [S2]

For a possible fraudulent charge or transfer, preserve the payment record and ask the provider how to report it. Do not rely on a refund notice from the same person or account that initiated the suspected scam, because the FTC press release does not establish that any particular scam victim is owed or eligible for a payment. [S1]

The FTC and CFPB resources allow consumers to move from a warning to a written, documented record. The most useful next step is usually independent verification before a payment or disclosure becomes irreversible. [S1][S2][S3]
