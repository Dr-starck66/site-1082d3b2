## What changed

Payment processor Nuvei and its subsidiaries will pay $4.85 million to settle Federal Trade Commission charges that they opened and maintained accounts for merchants they knew or should have known were engaged in deception. The FTC says the alleged activity included tech support scams that took millions of dollars from consumers. The announcement identifies Nuvei Corporation as Canada-based. [S1]

The FTC says Nuvei must also implement robust merchant-screening practices. That is a requirement aimed at how the processor screens merchants; it is not, by itself, a promise that a particular customer will get money back or that every questionable merchant account has been identified. [S1]

The figure in the headline is the amount Nuvei will pay to settle the FTC’s charges. The supplied announcement does not describe this payment as a consumer refund fund or say that consumers can apply to receive part of it. Consumers should not assume that the $4.85 million will be distributed to them. [S1]

The FTC’s announcement describes allegations and a settlement. For consumers, the practical starting point is still their own account records: identify the business name and amount on a charge, then compare those details with receipts, subscriptions, or other records. The information supplied here does not say which individual merchants or transactions were involved. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If a charge looks unfamiliar, first check whether the name on the account statement differs from the name of the shop, service, or website you remember using. Save the statement entry and any receipt, order confirmation, cancellation message, or conversation with the seller. This is a practical way to organize what happened; the FTC announcement does not establish that a specific charge was processed by Nuvei.

Next, contact the business using contact information you can independently verify and ask it to identify the transaction. If you still do not recognize or agree with the charge, contact the bank or card issuer that maintains the account and ask what process it provides for reporting it. Keep a record of when you reached out and any case or reference number. The supplied sources do not set out a deadline or guarantee a particular result, so ask the institution directly what timing and documentation apply.

A processor’s name may not be the name a consumer recognizes from a purchase. But the available FTC extract does not list merchants, transaction descriptors, or a lookup method for determining whether a particular payment went through Nuvei. Do not treat a similar-sounding name or an unfamiliar payment entry as proof of a connection to the case. [S1]

The settlement also does not establish that a consumer’s credit report has been affected. If you are concerned about your credit record, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau recommends checking credit reports at least once a year for errors that could lower a score, affect credit qualification, or affect loan terms. Requesting your credit reports will not hurt your credit score, according to the CFPB. [S2]

If you find information on a credit report that you believe is wrong, the CFPB provides a guide to disputing an error. Review the information and use the relevant reporting company’s process rather than assuming that a payment dispute and a credit-report dispute are the same issue. The supplied CFPB page points consumers to dispute guidance but does not specify a resolution or timeline for an individual case. [S2]

If someone contacts you to collect a debt connected to a disputed transaction, the CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, and how to dispute the debt, among other information. Use that information to check whether the debt is yours; the CFPB warns that a collector who will not or cannot provide it could be a scam. Don’t ignore a contact simply because you are unsure: the CFPB says ignoring a debt collector is unlikely to make the contact stop. [S3]

The CFPB also lists options for consumers who believe they have been harmed, including submitting a complaint. That can be an escalation route when you cannot resolve a consumer-finance issue directly, but the supplied material does not say that filing a complaint will secure a refund or determine whether a transaction is linked to Nuvei. [S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

- **The transaction:** Match the date and amount to your own purchase records. Ask the seller to identify the charge if the statement name is unfamiliar.
- **The route for a dispute:** Ask your bank or card issuer how it handles a charge you do not recognize or agree with, what information it needs, and what timing applies. The sources here do not provide a dispute deadline.
- **Any claimed connection to Nuvei:** The FTC extract does not name merchants or identify individual transactions. Verify with the business and your financial institution rather than relying on a name alone. [S1]
- **Your credit file:** If you are worried about an error, check your credit reports and use the CFPB’s dispute guidance. Requesting a report does not hurt your credit score. [S2]
- **A collection contact:** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount, and dispute instructions. If the information is missing or the debt does not look familiar, verify it before paying or sharing sensitive information. [S3]
- **The settlement itself:** Do not treat the $4.85 million as money owed to you. The supplied FTC announcement does not describe a consumer claim process or say that this payment will be distributed to customers. [S1]
