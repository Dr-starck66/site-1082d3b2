## What changed

The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement describes ongoing work, not a new consumer payment program or a promise that people who lost money will be reimbursed. [S1]

The FTC also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That context helps explain why the agency is highlighting enforcement, but it does not tell an individual whether a particular call, message, or website is genuine. [S1]

One especially important warning is directed at people contacted by someone claiming to represent the FTC: the agency says it will never threaten you, say you must transfer money to “protect it,” or tell you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. Those demands are a scam signal, not a legitimate way to safeguard funds. [S2]

The announcement is therefore best read as a report on agency activity paired with a practical warning—not as notice that consumers should expect a check, reply to an outreach message, or act on an unsolicited offer. The FTC’s consumer site separately provides a way to report fraud and points people who may have shared personal information toward identity-theft reporting. [S1][S2]

## What this means for consumers

If a caller, text, email, or website claims to be the FTC, start with the request being made. A demand to move money, withdraw cash, or purchase gold is enough reason to stop the interaction. Do not treat official-sounding language, a logo, or a website that appears polished as proof of identity; the agency says it has removed websites designed to impersonate it. [S1][S2]

Use a simple decision path:

- **Being told to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold?** Do not do it. The FTC says it does not make these demands to protect your money. End the contact rather than negotiating or asking the person to prove their identity. [S2]
- **Being asked to click a link or provide information?** Pause. The FTC’s announcement confirms that fake agency websites exist, so don’t rely on a link supplied by the person contacting you as evidence that you are dealing with the agency. [S1]
- **Already sent money or shared personal information?** Go to the FTC’s official Consumer Advice site independently and use its fraud or identity-theft reporting options as appropriate. The site lists both options and links to advice for people who think they were scammed or shared information. [S2]
- **Seeing a claim about a refund or redress?** Treat the agency’s reported enforcement total as an agency-level figure, not proof that you qualify for money. The announcement says the FTC secured more than $80 million in consumer redress; it does not say that every consumer is eligible or explain a claim process in the supplied information. [S1]

That last distinction matters. “Secured” redress describes the result the FTC reports from enforcement actions; it is not the same as a guarantee that money has already reached every affected person. The available announcement does not identify particular cases, eligible consumers, payment dates, or instructions for claiming funds. A message that fills in those missing details should be checked independently before you act. [S1]

For practical purposes, separate the contact into two questions: **Is it asking me to do something risky now?** and **Can I verify the claim through an official channel I reached myself?** If the first answer is yes, stop and do not send money or hand over cash or gold. If you have already acted, use the FTC’s reporting pathways rather than relying on the person who contacted you to resolve it. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a supposed FTC contact or a redress offer, check:

- **The exact demand:** Is anyone threatening you or directing you to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy gold? The FTC says it will not do these things. [S2]
- **The website:** Did you reach it independently, or did the contact provide the link? The FTC says it removed 19 websites impersonating the agency, so a site’s appearance alone should not settle the question. [S1]
- **The payment claim:** Does the message name a specific case, eligibility criteria, or deadline? The supplied announcement does not provide those details, so do not assume they are genuine based on the reported redress total. [S1]
- **Your next step:** If you paid or disclosed information, use the FTC’s official fraud or identity-theft reporting options. [S2]

The FTC’s announcement shows that enforcement against impersonators is ongoing, but consumers still need to judge each contact on its own merits. The safest immediate response to a demand for money is to stop; the next step, if you have already paid or shared information, is to report the incident through an official FTC channel reached independently.
