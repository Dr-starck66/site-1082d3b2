## What changed

In an October 29 update, the Federal Trade Commission said it had removed 19 websites impersonating the agency. It also reported that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency described these as highlights of ongoing efforts to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

Those figures describe agency actions and redress across enforcement cases; they do not say that every person who reports a scam will receive money, or that a new payment is available to consumers now. The announcement also does not identify a particular application process or deadline for claiming redress. Treat any separate message promising you money in the FTC’s name as a claim to check—not proof that you qualify. [S1][S2]

The FTC said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. That is a statement about reports and reported losses, not a measure of every scam or an estimate of what any one consumer will lose. [S1]

The agency’s latest update lands alongside another reminder about impersonation: scammers may pretend to represent a familiar charity and tell someone who has donated that they won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes. The FTC’s consumer advice flags that scenario as a reason to question whether the prize is real. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Start with what the caller, message, or website wants you to do. If someone claiming to be from the FTC threatens you, says you must move money to “protect it,” tells you to withdraw cash, or asks you to buy gold and give it to someone, the FTC says that is a scam. Don’t follow those instructions. [S2]

A quick decision path can help when a pitch feels urgent:

- **Is the person claiming to represent the FTC?** If the pitch includes a threat or asks you to transfer money, withdraw cash, or buy and hand over gold, stop. Those are warning signs the FTC specifically identifies. [S2]
- **Is the pitch about a prize from a charity you support?** Don’t treat the caller’s claim—or your past donation—as proof that the sweepstakes is real. The FTC warns that scammers use this connection to make a fake prize seem believable. [S2]
- **Have you already responded or shared information?** The FTC’s consumer advice page provides a “What To Do if You Were Scammed” article and links to report fraud and identity theft. Use those resources to identify an appropriate next step for what happened. [S2]

The agency’s enforcement figures show that it has taken action against imposter websites and obtained consumer redress in enforcement cases. They are not a reason to assume that a suspicious website is legitimate because it mentions refunds, or that reporting a loss guarantees reimbursement. Keep the two questions separate: whether the contact is trustworthy, and whether you might be eligible for any specific redress process. [S1][S2]

If you suspect an impersonation, preserve the message or website details so you can describe what happened when reporting it. The FTC’s consumer advice site offers a report-fraud option; it also lists report-identity-theft guidance for cases involving personal information. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before sending money, sharing personal information, or following instructions from someone claiming to represent an agency or charity, check the exact claim—not just the name or logo the person uses. For an FTC-related contact, compare the request with the agency’s warning: it says it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to protect it, or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it over. [S2]

For a supposed charity sweepstakes, verify that the charity actually sponsored the contest and that the caller’s description matches what the charity says. Don’t rely on the caller’s assertion that you won because you previously donated; the FTC specifically warns that scammers may use that detail to make a pitch convincing. [S2]

If the message says you are owed money because of an FTC case, check for a specific case and official instructions before responding. The FTC’s announcement reports more than $80 million in redress from enforcement actions since the rule took effect, but the supplied update does not establish that a particular recipient is eligible or explain how to claim a payment. [S1]

When in doubt, don’t act under pressure. Use the FTC’s consumer advice site to report fraud or identity theft, and review its guidance on scams and imposters. A report can give the agency information; it is not, by itself, a promise of a refund or other outcome. [S2]
