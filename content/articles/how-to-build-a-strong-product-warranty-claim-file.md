## Why your records matter

A warranty is a promise by a manufacturer or seller to stand behind a product. Its coverage can be broad or narrow, so a successful claim depends in part on identifying what was promised and what the written terms say. The Federal Trade Commission says warranty details should be in writing and that a warranty must be available to read before purchase, whether you shop in person or online. [S1]

A receipt alone may not explain what repairs are covered, how long coverage lasts, or what steps a customer must take. The warranty document alone may not show when and where you bought the item, or what a salesperson said about it. Keep both, along with any written messages about the product or coverage. This is a recordkeeping strategy, not a guarantee that a company will approve a claim.

Think of your file as a way to answer three questions: What did you buy? What promise are you relying on? And what happened when the product stopped working? Having those answers in one place can make it easier to describe the problem accurately and follow the company’s stated claim process.

## Start with the promise you are claiming

Before contacting the company, identify the specific promise you believe applies. It could be a written manufacturer’s warranty, a seller’s written warranty, or a separate service contract. For a car, the FTC distinguishes a warranty—which promises to fix certain defects or malfunctions during a specified period—from an optional auto service contract, which covers repairs or services set out in that contract. [S3]

Do not assume that a product advertisement, sales conversation, warranty, and service contract all provide identical coverage. Save the version of the document that applied when you bought the product, and note the exact sentence or section you think covers the issue. If a representative made a written promise that differs from the general warranty document, keep that message too and ask the company to explain how it applies.

For an auto purchase, check whether the document is actually a warranty or an optional service contract marketed as an “extended warranty.” The FTC says service contracts are sold by manufacturers, dealers, or independent companies; their prices and coverage vary, and some may overlap with the vehicle’s existing warranty. [S3]

## Build a product-and-purchase record

Create one folder for the item, on paper or digitally. Save copies rather than relying on an account portal, inbox, or paper slip as the only record. A simple file name—such as “Washer purchase and warranty”—makes it easier to locate documents later.

Include the purchase receipt or invoice, order confirmation, delivery or pickup record, and payment confirmation if available. Note the seller, purchase date, product name, model and serial number. If you cannot find a receipt, look for an order-history page, confirmation email, bank or card statement, or a duplicate from the seller. These records can help you describe and document the transaction; they do not, by themselves, establish that a particular repair is covered.

Save the warranty document and any related materials you received with the product. If the warranty says you must return a registration card to qualify for service, retain a copy of the completed card and proof of submission. The FTC notes that some warranties may require product registration before warranty service. [S1]

Keep written promises in their original form where possible: emails, chat transcripts, letters, product-page screenshots, and messages from the seller or manufacturer. Capture the date and the sender or page address when you can. If a promise was made verbally, write down who said it, when, and what you understood; distinguish your own note from a message created by the company. Do not present your recollection as a written company statement.

Photograph the product label and the problem. For a malfunction, record what it does, when the issue began, and any error message. If the issue changes over time, take dated photos or short videos. Keep these observations factual: “the display went blank after 10 minutes” is more useful than a conclusion such as “the motor is defective” unless a technician has confirmed the cause.

## Step-by-step: prepare and submit the claim

1. **Find the controlling documents.** Start with the written warranty and any separate service contract. Check the product, parts or repairs covered, the duration, any mileage limit, conditions, and instructions for requesting service. The FTC advises consumers to check coverage limitations and notes that if a specific part or repair is not listed as covered, consumers should assume it is not covered. [S1]

2. **Check the coverage period and any conditions.** Compare the product’s purchase date and, if relevant, mileage with the period or limit in the document. Look for any stated registration requirement or other condition. Do not infer a deadline or coverage period from a salesperson’s estimate when the written document gives different terms; ask the company to clarify the difference in writing. Warranty length and coverage can vary. [S1]

3. **Describe the failure precisely.** Record when the issue first appeared, what the product was doing, and what troubleshooting or service has already occurred. Include only details you can support. If there was an accident or other event that might be relevant, describe it accurately rather than omitting it.

4. **Contact the party named in the warranty.** Use the contact channel and claim instructions in the warranty or service contract. Provide the product identification, proof of purchase, a concise description, and copies of relevant written promises. Keep the originals. Ask for a claim or case number, the next step, and a written explanation if the company says the problem is outside coverage.

5. **Follow instructions and keep a timeline.** Note the date, method, and person or department contacted, plus what was requested and what the company said. Save emails and upload confirmations. If you speak by phone, write a brief same-day summary and send a follow-up message confirming your understanding. Keep copies of any service report, estimate, or decision.

6. **Pause before authorizing paid work.** If the company says the repair is not covered, ask what term it is relying on and whether an inspection or written diagnosis is available. Check whether the proposed work is being handled under the warranty, an optional service contract, or as a customer-paid repair. Ask what charges may apply before agreeing. This helps keep a coverage dispute separate from a decision to purchase repair work.

## Read exclusions and limits carefully

A warranty does not necessarily cover every situation or repair. The FTC advises checking which parts and repairs are covered and asking what limitations apply. The terms may also set a particular coverage period or mileage limit, and some warranties require registration before service. [S1]

Do not treat silence as a promise of coverage. If a component, repair, or type of damage is not listed, the FTC’s consumer guidance says to assume it is not covered. Ask the company for the relevant section in writing rather than relying on a broad description such as “full coverage.” [S1]

For a vehicle, distinguish a defect or malfunction claim from a request involving routine maintenance, accident damage, or normal wear. The FTC’s auto guidance says warranties cover certain defects or malfunctions for a specific period, while service contracts can cover specified repairs or services and may include some maintenance. It also says service contracts typically do not cover accident damage or normal wear and tear. [S3]

If the product was used, do not assume the same coverage as a new item. The FTC says a used vehicle might come with some type of warranty coverage; the actual terms need to be checked in the documents for that purchase. [S3]

## If the company denies or delays the claim

First, compare the explanation with the exact warranty language and your evidence. Ask the company to identify the clause it relied on, explain which fact it believes is missing, and state whether it needs additional documents or an inspection. A clear written response can help you decide whether the disagreement is about timing, a coverage exclusion, a registration condition, the cause of the failure, or the identity of the party responsible under the document.

If a seller made a written promise, send the relevant message with the claim and ask the seller to address it directly. Keep the request calm and specific: identify the product, summarize the issue, quote the promise accurately, and state what resolution you are requesting. Do not assume that a seller’s statement changes the written warranty; ask the business to explain its position.

If the initial contact does not resolve the matter, use the company’s stated escalation channel, such as a supervisor or customer-relations department, and include the case number and a short timeline. The FTC advises consumers to report problems with products, and its warranty page points consumers to problem-resolution information. Reporting a problem is an option, not a promise of an individual claim decision. [S1]

You can also check with your local or state consumer protection office for information about a company. The FTC suggests this as one way to learn about a warranty provider, alongside searching the company’s name with terms such as “complaint” or “review.” [S1]

## Warranty claim checklist

Before you send a claim, check that your file includes:

- The receipt, invoice, order record, or other purchase documentation you have.
- The warranty document and any separate service contract.
- Product name, model and serial number, plus the seller and purchase date.
- Any written sales promise, email, chat, advertisement, or product-page record relevant to the issue.
- A short factual description of the malfunction and when it began.
- Photos, video, error messages, diagnostic findings, or service reports, if available.
- A record of registration if the warranty requires it.
- The exact warranty section you believe applies and any coverage limit or exclusion that may be relevant.
- A copy of your claim, the case number, and a dated contact log.

If a document is missing, say so plainly and ask the seller or manufacturer whether it can provide a copy. If you cannot locate proof of purchase, gather alternate records and ask what documentation the company accepts. Do not assume that a missing receipt automatically ends a claim—or that alternate records will be accepted—because the supplied warranty terms may set requirements that need to be checked directly.

## What to verify before you act

Read the actual warranty or service contract for the product you bought. Confirm who made the promise, what is covered, when coverage ends, whether a mileage limit applies, and whether registration or another stated condition is required. Warranty terms and coverage vary, and the FTC’s guidance calls out these limits as questions consumers should check. [S1]

Before paying for diagnosis or repair, ask the company whether the proposed step is required for a warranty claim and whether charges could apply. Confirm in writing whether the company considers the issue covered, what term supports its decision, and what options remain under the document. Do not treat this article as a substitute for the terms of your specific warranty or service contract.

Keep your evidence together from purchase through resolution. A clear file cannot guarantee coverage, but it can help you present the promise, product history, and claim consistently—and see what needs to be verified before deciding what to do next.
