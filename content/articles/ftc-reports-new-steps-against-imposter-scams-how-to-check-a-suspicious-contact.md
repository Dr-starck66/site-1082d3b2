The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The announcement is a reminder to treat unexpected contact claiming to be from the government, a charity or another familiar organization with care—and to verify before sending money or personal information. [S1]

## What changed

In an October 29 update, the FTC described those website removals and enforcement results as part of its ongoing effort to combat impersonation scams. The agency also said imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. [S1]

The figures show enforcement activity, not a promise that a person who has lost money will receive a refund. The announcement does not provide details in the supplied information about how individual cases are selected for redress, who may qualify, or how much any one consumer might receive. Treat the total as a report on agency actions, not as an offer or an estimate of your own recovery. [S1]

The FTC’s consumer advice also points to a common tactic: a scammer may impersonate a charity, or claim that someone has won a sweepstakes sponsored by a charity they support. That kind of message can sound plausible because it refers to an organization the recipient recognizes. Familiarity alone, however, does not verify who made contact. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

Start with the request, not the name or logo attached to it. If someone claiming to represent the FTC says you must move money to protect it, withdraw cash, or buy gold and hand it over, the FTC says that is a scam. Do not follow those instructions. [S2]

For other unexpected messages, pause before responding. If a caller says you won a charity-sponsored sweepstakes, for example, don’t rely on the caller’s claim or on a link, number, or reply address they provide. Instead, look up the organization’s contact information independently and ask whether the message is genuine. This is a practical verification step—not proof that a prize exists or that you are eligible to receive one. [S2]

Use the same pause when a message appears to come from a government agency. The FTC’s site explains that official federal government websites often end in .gov or .mil, and advises people to make sure they are on a government site before sharing sensitive information. A familiar-looking page or message is not enough on its own; check the web address before entering details. [S1]

If a request pressures you to act immediately, you can choose not to engage while you verify it. Don’t send money or sensitive information just to stop a threat or secure a supposed prize. Contact the organization through a channel you found independently, and explain what the message asked you to do. If you can’t verify the claim, treat it as unconfirmed rather than taking the sender’s word for it. [S1][S2]

If you already paid or shared information, avoid making another payment to someone who says they can recover the money or fix the problem unless you have independently verified who they are. The FTC’s consumer advice page offers a “What To Do if You Were Scammed” article and links to report fraud and identity theft. Those resources are reasonable next stops for deciding what steps fit your situation; reporting does not, by itself, establish eligibility for redress. [S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, replying, or sharing information, check three things: who is contacting you, whether the request is real, and where any link leads. Find the organization’s official site or contact details independently rather than relying on what came with the message. For a claimed FTC contact, remember the agency’s specific warning: it will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to protect it, or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone. [S2]

If the sender claims you are owed money because of an FTC case, verify the claim using the FTC’s official website and its refund-program information. The supplied announcement reports more than $80 million in consumer redress across enforcement actions, but it does not identify a particular recipient or say that every person contacted is entitled to money. Don’t treat an unsolicited promise of payment as confirmation. [S1]

When facts remain unclear, stop and check before acting. You can use the FTC’s consumer advice site to find scam guidance or report fraud; if you’re unsure whether a message is genuine, don’t let urgency substitute for verification. [S2]
