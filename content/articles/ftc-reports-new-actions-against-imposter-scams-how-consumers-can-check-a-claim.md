The Federal Trade Commission says it removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and that enforcement actions have resulted in more than $80 million in consumer redress since the FTC’s Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency also says imposter scams have been the top reported fraud category over the past several years, with consumers reporting billions of dollars in losses. Those figures describe agency actions and reported losses—not a promise that any individual will receive money back. [S1]

For consumers dealing with credit or debt, the practical question is often whether a message about a debt, payment, or supposed refund is genuine. A message that uses an agency’s name or a familiar creditor’s name is not, on its own, proof of who sent it. Pause before paying or sharing personal information, and check the claim through a separate, trusted route. [S1][S3]

## What changed

In an October 29, 2026 announcement, the FTC highlighted its efforts to combat impersonation scams, including the removal of 19 websites that impersonated the FTC. The agency said enforcement actions have led to more than $80 million in consumer redress since its Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. [S1]

The FTC’s announcement frames these steps against a broader problem: it says consumers have reported billions of dollars in losses to imposter scams, which have been the most reported fraud category over the past several years. The supplied announcement does not break down those losses by scam type or say how many people received redress. [S1]

That distinction matters when a message claims to be connected to the FTC’s work. A real agency action does not establish that a specific call, text, website, debt, or refund offer is legitimate. Nor does the announcement say that every person contacted by a scammer is eligible for payment. [S1]

## What this means for consumers

If someone claiming to collect a debt contacts you, use the conversation to find out what the debt is. The CFPB says a debt collector is required to provide the creditor’s name, the amount owed, information about how to dispute the debt, and more; if the person will not or cannot provide that information, it could be a scam. [S3]

A useful decision path is to pause, request the debt details, and check whether they match your records before deciding what to do. If information is missing or does not make sense, do not treat urgency or an official-sounding name as verification. The CFPB recommends telling a collector if you believe you do not owe the debt, and its resources explain how to reply or dispute a debt. [S3]

If a message claims you are owed money because of an FTC case, verify the case and any payment information independently rather than relying on links or contact details in the message. The FTC announcement reports aggregate redress from enforcement actions; it does not establish your eligibility or provide a method for claiming money. [S1]

Credit records can help you spot a problem, but checking them is not a substitute for confirming a caller’s identity. The CFPB recommends reviewing credit reports at least once a year for errors that could affect a credit score, loan qualification, or loan terms. It also says requesting your reports will not hurt your credit score and points consumers to a list of credit reporting companies. [S2]

If you find an unfamiliar account or other information that looks wrong, the CFPB provides instructions for disputing a credit report error. If the issue is a debt collection contact, its debt-collection resources cover communicating with collectors and what to do if you believe the debt is not yours. [S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Before paying, clicking, or sharing personal or financial information, check:

- **Who contacted you:** Find the organization’s official contact information independently and ask whether it sent the message. Do not use a link or phone number from a message you already suspect may be fake.
- **What debt is involved:** Ask for the creditor’s name, amount, and how to dispute the debt. Compare the details with your own records; the CFPB says missing information or a refusal to provide it could indicate a scam. [S3]
- **Whether your credit records show a problem:** Review your reports and use the CFPB’s guidance if you see information you believe is inaccurate. Requesting your credit reports will not hurt your score. [S2]
- **Whether a promised payment is actually yours:** Confirm any claimed case or redress through an independently located official source. The FTC’s reported redress total does not confirm that a particular offer is real or that you qualify. [S1]

If the debt remains unclear, use the CFPB’s debt-collection guidance to understand how to reply and dispute it. If your credit report contains an error, follow the CFPB’s dispute guidance. These are practical next steps, not guarantees of a particular outcome. [S2][S3]

## What to verify before you act

Treat the FTC’s announcement as a warning about impersonation and a report of agency activity—not as proof that an individual message is genuine. Verify the sender, the debt, and any claimed redress through independent channels before you make a payment or share information. The CFPB’s resources can help you check debt details and review or dispute credit-report information. [S1][S2][S3]
