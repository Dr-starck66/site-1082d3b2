The Federal Trade Commission says it has removed 19 websites impersonating the agency and secured more than $80 million in consumer redress through enforcement actions since its Impersonation Rule took effect in 2024. The agency shared those figures on October 29 as part of an update on its efforts to combat impersonation scams. [S1]

The announcement is a reminder that scammers can pose as the FTC itself. The agency’s consumer advice draws a clear line around what it will not do: it will not threaten you, tell you to transfer money to “protect it,” or direct you to withdraw cash or buy gold and hand it to someone. [S2]

## What changed

The FTC’s update describes enforcement activity and the removal of websites pretending to be the agency. It also reports more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since the Impersonation Rule went into effect in 2024. The release does not say that every person contacted by an impostor is eligible for money, or that the total represents a new payment program. [S1]

That distinction matters. An agency-wide redress figure is not the same as a notice that a particular consumer is owed money. The FTC’s consumer site separately provides a way to report fraud and access consumer advice, but the supplied information does not identify a specific claims process connected to this announcement. [S1][S2]

The FTC also provides a direct warning about impostors: a demand to transfer money for safekeeping, withdraw cash, or purchase gold and hand it over is not a legitimate instruction from the agency. Threats are another warning sign. [S2]

## What this means for consumers

If someone claiming to be from the FTC contacts you, pause before sending money or sharing information. First ask whether the person is using a threat or telling you to move money, withdraw cash, or buy gold for someone else. If so, do not follow that instruction; the FTC says it will not make those demands. [S2]

If the contact does not include those exact demands, that alone does not establish that it is genuine. Treat the caller’s identity as unverified. Rather than relying on a number, link, or instructions supplied in the message, go to the FTC’s official website yourself. The agency identifies its official web address as ftc.gov and explains that government websites generally use .gov domains and secure connections use https. [S1][S2]

Next, separate the immediate safety question from any question about getting money back. The announcement reports enforcement outcomes in aggregate; it does not promise a refund or say that a consumer can claim part of the reported redress simply by responding to an unsolicited message. Do not pay a fee or provide account access because someone says a payout is waiting. [S1]

If you believe you encountered a scam, the FTC’s consumer site offers a way to report fraud and a separate option to report identity theft. It also links to consumer advice, including an article on what to do if you paid someone you think is a scammer or shared personal information or access to a device. Use those official resources to identify next steps for your situation; reporting does not, by itself, guarantee a refund or other outcome. [S2]

A practical decision path is: stop if the contact threatens you or directs you to move money, withdraw cash, or buy gold; independently verify the agency’s website rather than following the sender’s route; then use the FTC’s reporting or identity-theft resources if you think you were targeted or shared information. If money or access has already been handed over, focus on the steps that apply to what you shared or paid, rather than assuming the FTC’s reported redress covers your loss. [S1][S2]

## What to verify before you act

Before responding to a message or call claiming to be from the FTC, check these points:

- **What are they asking you to do?** A threat, a demand to transfer money to “protect it,” or instructions to withdraw cash or buy gold and give it to someone are reasons to stop. The FTC says it will not make those demands. [S2]
- **Where did the contact send you?** Do not treat a familiar-looking name or website as proof of identity. Navigate independently to ftc.gov, the agency’s official website, instead of relying on a link in an unexpected message. [S1][S2]
- **Are they promising money?** The FTC’s reported figure is more than $80 million in consumer redress from enforcement actions since 2024; the release does not establish that you personally qualify or describe a new claims process. Verify any supposed payment through official FTC information before acting. [S1]
- **What did you already share or pay?** The FTC’s consumer site has reporting options for fraud and identity theft, and advice for people who paid a suspected scammer or shared information or device access. Choose the resource that matches what happened. [S2]

The central takeaway is not that every contact using the FTC’s name is easy to identify from a headline. It is that the agency has warned against specific demands, reported enforcement against impersonation, and made consumer reporting and advice available through its own site. Verify the source independently, and do not treat an unsolicited promise of money as proof that a payment is owed. [S1][S2]
