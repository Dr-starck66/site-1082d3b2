# Cahors Wine: How to Choose a French Malbec for Dinner or a Trade Order

Cahors is a red-wine appellation in the Lot department of southwest France, built around Malbec, also called Côt. The name identifies an origin and production rules; it does not tell you whether a particular bottle is ready tonight, heavily oaked or suitable for your buying budget. Those answers require a few more precise questions.

## What the Cahors name actually tells you

The official Vins de Cahors organisation describes an appellation reserved for red wines, with Malbec making up at least 70% of the blend. Merlot and Tannat are the permitted accompanying varieties. A Cahors can therefore be entirely Malbec, but the appellation name alone does not establish that it is. Ask for the actual blend of the specific cuvée and vintage. Keep three pieces of information separate: Cahors is the appellation, Malbec is the grape, and the estate or producer is the supplier. A bottle labelled Malbec from elsewhere is not automatically Cahors. Equally, a producer located near Cahors may also sell wines under another designation. The complete label and technical sheet are better evidence than a retailer's short category name. 

## The Lot valley and the causses: useful context, not a tasting shortcut

The regional interprofessional body distinguishes two broad landscape settings: terraces associated with the Lot and the limestone causses above them. The Occitanie vineyard organisation also describes terraces, plateaus and variation in vineyard position. These features help explain why the appellation contains different expressions of the same leading grape. For a purchase, use this geography to ask better questions rather than to assign a score. Request the vineyard origin, the producer's intended style and the ageing method. Do not conclude that a plateau wine must be better, that a valley wine will always be softer, or that every bottle has a fixed flavour. Site, season and winemaking decisions interact. A producer's description is still a claim about its own wine; it becomes more useful when accompanied by a technical sheet and, for a larger order, a representative sample. 

## Choosing a bottle for tonight

Start with the meal and the people sharing it. Someone who enjoys a lighter, fruit-led red has a different brief from someone seeking a structured wine for a rich stew. Tell the merchant which outcome you want, then ask whether the proposed bottle is intended for early drinking or further cellaring. An older date on the label is not sufficient evidence of readiness or good storage. For slow-cooked dishes, a practical approach is to compare the sauce, salt, richness and accompaniments, rather than matching only the word beef or duck. For a mixed table, ask for an approachable style and avoid buying solely from a description such as powerful. ## Young drinking or cellaring: ask for evidence

Vignobles du Sud-Ouest describes both wines intended to be enjoyed young and more ambitious examples made for ageing. That distinction is more helpful than treating Cahors as one uniform cellar wine. For a bottle you plan to keep, ask the producer for its suggested drinking window for the exact vintage, the expected style of evolution and the storage assumptions behind that advice. For a bottle already several years old, provenance matters. Ask where it has been stored, whether the seller acquired it directly, and whether the condition of the closure and fill level can be checked. No general guide can guarantee that an individual bottle will improve. A small comparison purchase can reduce uncertainty before committing to a case, and written answers are especially useful when the order is intended for resale. 

## A comparison table that improves a buying conversation

Use the same questions for each candidate bottle. This avoids comparing a detailed estate technical sheet with a vague marketplace description. Leave a cell blank when the information is missing, and ask the seller to complete it rather than inferring a value from the appellation. The most useful result is not necessarily a winner. You may discover that one wine suits immediate restaurant service while another suits a retail customer who wants to cellar it. A comparison should make those different uses visible. Price, alcohol level, certification, stock and minimum order must be verified for the actual product; none is supplied by the landscape photograph or the regional name. 

## For trade buyers: compare the complete order, not just the bottle price

An ex-cellar quote is a starting point. Request the exact cuvée and vintage, bottle size, case configuration, minimum order, available quantity, quote currency, tax treatment, collection address and validity date. Then establish who arranges transport and which responsibilities are included. A lower unit price may be less useful if the quantity, collection conditions or lead time do not fit the order. For a United States enquiry, keep product selection separate from import compliance. Confirm the authorised importer, applicable labelling requirements and the responsibilities of each participant before placing an order. ## A six-question Cahors buying brief

Write a short brief before requesting recommendations: what is the occasion or sales channel; when will the wine be opened; what style does the buyer prefer; how many bottles are needed; where must the order arrive; and what is the budget on a clearly stated cost basis? That brief allows a seller to answer a concrete request rather than send an unrelated catalogue.
