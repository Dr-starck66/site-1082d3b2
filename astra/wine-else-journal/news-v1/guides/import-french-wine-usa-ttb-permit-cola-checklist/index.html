Importing French Wine into the USA: TTB Permits, COLA and a Buyer Checklist

A French wine bottle does not become a US retail product simply because a carrier can transport it. This practical checklist separates the licensed importer, TTB label approval, customs, FDA obligations and state distribution—before anyone quotes a landed price.

Key distinction. A licensed US importer is not the same as a freight carrier or a state distributor. A TTB COLA is a label approval, not a purchase order or a guarantee of state-level sales permission.

Start with the US importer, not the freight quote

Commercially importing wine into the United States is a regulated business. The US Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau (TTB) explains that an entity carrying out this business generally needs a Federal Basic Importer’s Permit. An applicant seeking that permit must maintain and staff a business office in the United States. A French grower or an overseas wine sourcing intermediary should not assume that an attractive transport offer, a US mailing address or an interested retailer substitutes for the legal importer.

For a foreign supplier that cannot conduct the importing business in the US, TTB expressly describes contracting with an existing licensed importer as an alternative. This can avoid the foreign company seeking its own importer permit, but it does not eliminate the need for the licensed US party to accept the product, documents, commercial terms and regulatory responsibilities. A freight forwarder and a licensed importer may be different businesses; verify which legal entity is responsible for each function.

Understand the COLA before ordering labels

A Certificate of Label Approval, or COLA, is a TTB approval of a product label. TTB states that, for imported wine covered by the Federal Alcohol Administration Act, the importer must possess the relevant COLA when the product is imported. In the typical case of bottled wine at 7% alcohol by volume or more, label review is an essential step before release from customs custody for commercial consumption. It is not enough that a bottle already carries a lawful French or EU label.

TTB's published guidance distinguishes wine below 7% alcohol by volume, which is not subject to the same federal COLA preapproval requirement, from wines at or above that threshold. This does not mean lower-alcohol wine is unregulated: other TTB, FDA, customs and state requirements may apply. Importing wine in bulk is also a different case from importing bottles for retail sale. Ask the importer to classify the actual product rather than treating every wine shipment alike.

The label, the producer and the exact SKU must match

A useful product dossier identifies the legal producer, wine type, appellation or geographic indication, vintage where applicable, alcohol strength, bottle size, label artwork and any importer details required for the intended route to market. TTB's label guidance specifies mandatory information and a government health warning for covered wines. Some products may also require additional formula or pre-COLA review. The importer should check the specific requirements before the producer prints a US-labelled batch.

Do not assume that an existing COLA for one label or one importer automatically authorizes every revised label, product or importer. TTB publishes rules on allowable revisions, and some changes can be made without a new approval while others cannot. The practical question is whether the precise bottle and artwork intended for shipment are covered by the right approval and whether the importer can demonstrate its authority to use that approval.

FDA, customs and federal tax are separate checks

The TTB import guidance notes that businesses manufacturing, processing, packing or holding food, including alcoholic beverages, for US consumption may have FDA registration obligations. Imported food, including alcohol, can also be subject to FDA prior notice. These requirements are separate from a TTB basic permit or COLA. They must be assessed by the responsible parties for the actual shipment and facility, not assumed to be complete because a transport company can book air freight.

US Customs and Border Protection collects applicable federal excise taxes and customs duties, according to TTB. The importer must account for relevant customs entry, classification and documentation. Duty rates, taxes, surcharges and applicable trade measures can change, so an old landed-cost calculation should never be reused as a current quote. This guide does not assign a universal duty or shipping cost to French wine: the exact tariff and commercial terms must be verified for the transaction.

State-level distribution is not the same as federal importation

A bottle that may legally enter the United States is not automatically cleared for every downstream buyer. TTB warns that state and local authorities may impose additional requirements on beverage alcohol. A licensed importer may be able to arrange customs entry yet still need a separate compliant path to a wholesaler, distributor, retailer or other licensed buyer in the destination state.

Before quoting a buyer in Illinois, New York, California or any other state, identify the intended type of customer, the delivery state, the seller of record and the distributor or fulfillment partner where needed. A retail store, restaurant and wholesaler do not necessarily have the same purchasing and delivery rules. Do not promise direct-to-consumer delivery, interstate fulfillment or a retail sale on the basis of a federal import permit alone.

Ask for a complete commercial buyer brief

A serious sourcing conversation should start with a buyer brief, not a list of attractive estates. Record the target state and channel, wine styles and appellations, organic or other production preferences if relevant, target price expressed with a clear currency and Incoterm, annual volume, trial order, bottle format, preferred shipping method and desired timeline. Ask whether the buyer already has an importer of record or a distributor arrangement.

For a producer, request the precise cuvée and vintage, professional ex-cellar price excluding tax, stock or export allocation, minimum order, packaging configuration, readiness date, payment conditions and any existing US territorial rights. These are questions, not claims about inventory. A public retail price in euros cannot be treated as a producer's wholesale offer, and a target US shelf price cannot be converted into a verified margin without the intervening costs and contractual terms.

Build a landed-cost model without imaginary numbers

An ex-cellar quote is only one component of the final cost. Depending on the agreed Incoterm and actual service scope, the model may need inland collection, consolidation, export formalities, insurance, international freight, customs brokerage, federal duties and excise taxes, warehousing, state-level distribution and local delivery. Include breakage provisions, samples, currency conversion and the time for regulatory approvals where relevant.

Create a spreadsheet with each cost line, who pays it, the currency, the validity date and the source of the figure. Keep unknown items explicitly unknown. A margin is not demonstrated until a documented B2B sale price and all relevant purchase and logistics costs are available. For an initial test shipment, the unit cost can be very different from a full pallet; no single shipping price should be presented as representative of every transaction.

An efficient sequence for a small French producer

First, qualify a real buyer or import partner and define the destination state. Second, check that the importing entity has the appropriate TTB permit and is willing to act for this product. Third, assemble the producer dossier and have the importer verify the label, COLA and any other required approvals. Fourth, confirm the FDA and customs responsibilities and obtain a dated freight and landed-cost estimate. Fifth, document commercial terms, availability, insurance and payment before anyone dispatches bottles.

This order deliberately puts legal responsibility and buyer demand before speculative inventory. A producer should not reserve scarce stock or pay for US-specific packaging on the assumption that a buyer will eventually appear. A sourcing intermediary should not advertise a wine as available, exclusive or ready to ship without written evidence from the producer and the authorized US parties.

Common mistakes that delay an otherwise promising deal

One mistake is confusing a transport quote with a turnkey legal import service. Another is assuming the French label is sufficient for US retail distribution. A third is overlooking the difference between federal importation and the rules of the destination state. Other frequent planning errors include quoting tax-inclusive consumer prices as wholesale costs, failing to identify the importer of record and leaving a bottle's exact vintage or alcohol strength unspecified.

The corrective approach is a written responsibilities matrix. For every step—TTB permit, label, FDA, customs, transport, insurance, state distribution and invoicing—name the actual party responsible and the documentary proof expected. If an importer or distributor declines a particular state, that is a business constraint to resolve, not a reason to improvise a workaround.
