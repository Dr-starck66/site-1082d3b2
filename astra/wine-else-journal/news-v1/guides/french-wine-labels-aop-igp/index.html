French Wine Labels Explained: AOP vs IGP vs Vin de France

Understand AOP, IGP and Vin de France on French wine labels, what each classification means, which label details matter and how buyers can compare bottles.

By WINE ELSE Editorial Desk · Published 2 November 2026 · Evergreen educational guide

Three words that tell you how to read a French bottle

A French wine label can look like a compact geography lesson: a place name, a vintage, a producer, a château or domaine, and perhaps several abbreviations. For an international shopper or wine buyer, one distinction saves a great deal of confusion. AOP, IGP and Vin de France describe different legal approaches to origin and production, not a simple ladder from bad wine to good wine.

Knowing the distinction helps you understand where a wine is from, which production rules it follows, and what information you still need before buying. This guide focuses on French still wine and uses French and European official information rather than promotional rankings.

Quick comparison: AOP, IGP and Vin de France

At a glance

  • AOP (PDO): Protected Designation of Origin. The wine is linked to a defined place and must satisfy the corresponding production specification. In France, AOC is the historically familiar national designation used alongside the European AOP framework.
  • IGP (PGI): Protected Geographical Indication. There is a documented link to a defined geographic area, with a distinct legal specification. It can permit producers to work with a different range of styles and varieties than a particular AOP.
  • Vin de France: Wine without a protected geographic indication, under the broader national origin designation. It is not necessarily inexpensive or low quality; assess the producer, wine, vintage and factual information on the bottle.

These categories can be found in the French government's practical guide to wine labelling and the INAO's explanation of geographical indications. Their main function is to protect accurate descriptions of provenance and production, not to provide a universal tasting score.

What does AOP mean on a French wine label?

AOP stands for Appellation d'Origine Protégée, translated as Protected Designation of Origin, or PDO. The geographical name is protected. A wine bearing that name must conform to a documented specification, which can address the permitted production area, grape varieties, growing practices, yields, winemaking processes and other characteristics.

The key word is specific. There is no single, universal set of grape rules applying to every AOP. A producer of an AOP wine must follow the actual specification for that appellation. Two French appellations can therefore have different permitted grapes or methods even when located relatively close to one another.

You may also encounter AOC, or Appellation d'Origine Contrôlée. AOC is the French designation historically associated with appellation-controlled products; AOP is the European protection framework. Do not treat seeing AOC rather than AOP as proof that the bottle is a different wine classification or of a higher quality.

Useful examples for exploring a map are the Languedoc's Pic Saint-Loup, Corbières and Minervois. When comparing bottles, check the exact appellation named on the label and then consult its current official specification instead of assuming all Languedoc wines share the same recipe.

What is IGP wine, and how is it different?

IGP means Indication Géographique Protégée, the EU's Protected Geographical Indication (PGI). The INAO explains that an IGP identifies a product whose quality, reputation or other characteristics are linked to its geographical origin. For the wider IGP framework, at least one relevant production or processing stage is performed within the defined territory, subject to the sector-specific conditions.

IGP should not be understood as a guarantee that every single grape-growing or winemaking rule is relaxed, nor as a synonym for generic wine. It is its own protected geographical framework. Many buyers appreciate IGP wines because a producer may be able to present grape varieties or styles not permitted by a more tightly specified local AOP. The details still depend on the protected indication's own rules.

For a shopper, the next step is practical: find the geographical name associated with the IGP, identify the producer, check grape or blend information if provided, and read any technical notes rather than judging solely by the classification.

Is Vin de France a lower-quality wine?

No classification guarantees taste, craftsmanship or value. Vin de France is a French wine category without a protected geographical indication of the AOP or IGP kind. It should not be read as a negative quality assessment. Some producers deliberately choose a non-GI category to pursue a wine style that does not fit a specific appellation rule.

That does not make all such wines experimental or excellent either. The same sensible questions apply: Who made it? Which grapes are used, if stated? What is the vintage? Was the wine bottled by the producer or by another operator? Is there a technical sheet explaining its production and the available stock? Classification gives legal context; the actual bottle must still stand on its merits.

For buyers abroad, verify the exact wording of origin on the finished label. Do not replace a producer's description with an assumed regional origin unless the documented product information supports it.

Nine label details worth checking before you buy

The French government's DGCCRF consumer guidance explains the regulated labelling information that helps buyers understand and trace wine. Depending on the wine and applicable transitional or format provisions, a label can convey the following information directly on the bottle or, for certain details, via a permitted electronic label:

  1. Sales designation and geographical category — what the product is.
  2. Alcohol by volume — the declared alcoholic strength.
  3. Declared origin or provenance — the legally required origin information.
  4. Nominal volume — for example 750 ml.
  5. Bottler's or responsible operator's identification — important for traceability.
  6. Lot identification — enables a particular production batch to be traced.
  7. Allergen information — relevant to consumers with sensitivities.
  8. Ingredients — subject to applicable wine labelling requirements.
  9. Nutrition declaration — presented under the applicable information rules.

The details are not interchangeable: a picturesque producer name is not a substitute for a lot code, and a grape variety on the front label is not the same thing as a protected geographical indication. If an electronic ingredients or nutrition presentation is used, the responsible business should verify that it complies with the rules applying to that particular wine and market.

Does AOP tell you the grapes?

Not reliably. An AOP name identifies a regulated place and specification, not necessarily an explicit single-variety wine. Some wines are blends, and grapes may or may not appear prominently on the front label. The official specification and a producer's technical sheet are much stronger ways to establish what is inside a particular cuvée.

As a simple comparison, one red wine labelled with a protected southern appellation may follow a local blending specification, whereas a different wine in an IGP may carry a prominent grape name. Neither approach, by itself, tells you which wine tastes better. For more context, read our French wine grape guide and introduction to Languedoc reds.

Are AOP and IGP the same as organic wine?

No. The AOP and IGP systems primarily concern geographical origin and a specification connected with it. Organic production, sustainability claims, biodynamic certifications and environmental commitments are separate questions. Do not infer that a wine is certified organic, pesticide-free or carbon-neutral because its label carries AOP or IGP.

If environmental claims influence a buying decision, look for the relevant, verifiable certification and its scope. For importers and wholesale buyers, request current documentation and ensure that any certification claims will be lawful in the destination market.

How importers should read a French wine label

A professional buyer needs more than a consumer-facing explanation. The safest first step is to verify the label against the estate's current technical and commercial documents. Ask for the producer's complete legal name and contact details, the exact cuvée and vintage, AOP/IGP or other classification, grape or blend details, bottle size, actual available quantities and the destination-market compliance file.

Then request net ex-cellar or other clearly defined Incoterm pricing, minimum order quantity, export documentation, packaging specifications and lead times. These facts cannot be inferred from the appellation alone. A retail shelf price likewise does not establish the price at which a producer will sell to a distributor.

For the United States, importation and distribution require their own checks; a French label that is compliant domestically is not automatically approved for US sale. A specialist should review relevant TTB and other importer obligations for the exact transaction. Our US wine import checklist provides a starting point, not legal clearance.

Which classification should you choose?

For discovering a particular traditional French wine style, begin with an AOP appellation and understand its specification. For a grape-led or geographically linked alternative, explore IGP wines and compare individual producers. For wines offered as Vin de France, inspect their actual technical descriptions and the producer's intentions rather than assuming quality from the category.

For a curious consumer, buy by style, provenance, bottle condition and credible producer information. For an import buyer, compare evidence of supply, compliance and landed cost. Classification is a useful starting point, not the end of the buying decision.
